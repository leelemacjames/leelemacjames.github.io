Counting in powers of ten

How a graph with a stretched axis tells you how fast a tumour doubles, and how the same trick finds the hidden power in a crumpled ball of paper.

1 From a diameter to a number of cells

Doctors cannot count the cells in a tumour, but a scan can measure its diameter D. If we imagine both the tumour and each cell as little balls, the number of cells that fit inside is the ratio of their diameters multiplied by itself three times, because volume grows as length cubed:

N = (D / d)3

A cancer cell is about d = 10 micrometres across, and since one millimetre is 1000 micrometres, a 4 mm tumour is 400 cells wide, which means 400 × 400 × 400 = 64 million cells. Slide the hero control above and watch how quickly the count runs away from you, because every time the diameter doubles the number of cells multiplies by eight.

Tick the box in the panel above to use the spreadsheet's printed value, d = 10mm. A 4 mm lump then contains less than a tenth of one cell, which is impossible, and that nonsense answer is your first clue that the spreadsheet has lost a symbol somewhere. You will hunt this and five other errors in stage 5.

2 The graph that squashes its own data

Here are the six scans from the spreadsheet, with the patient's first scan counted as day 0. Plotted on ordinary axes, where each gridline adds the same amount, four of the six points appear to sit on the floor of the graph, even though the smallest of them is 64 million cells.

On a logarithmic axis each gridline is ten times the one below it, so equal steps up the page mean equal multiplications rather than equal additions. Once the axis is stretched the squashed points lift off the floor, and something surprising appears, because the six points now lie very nearly on a straight line.

3 What a logarithm actually measures

The logarithm of a number, written log10, is simply how many times you must multiply 1 by ten to reach it, so log10 1000 = 3 and log10 64 000 000 is about 7.81, which tells you that 64 million sits a little more than four-fifths of the way from ten million to a hundred million. Type any positive number to see where it lands on the ladder alongside the six tumour counts.

Try 64 000 000 and then 128 000 000, and notice that doubling always moves the marker the same distance, 0.301 of a step, whether you double a tiny number or an enormous one. That fixed distance is the whole reason a doubling process becomes a straight line on a log scale.

4 A straight line means a steady doubling time

The gold line is the straight line that fits the six points best on the log scale. Drag the time tracer and follow the two vertical markers: the rose one marks any day you choose, and the green one marks the day on which the line has reached exactly twice as many cells. However far along you slide, the gap between them never changes.

This tumour doubles roughly every 169 days, and it grows tenfold roughly every 561 days. A quantity that doubles in equal times is growing exponentially, and on a graph with a log vertical axis and an ordinary time axis, which is called a semi-log graph, exponential growth is always a straight line whose slope sets the doubling time.

Running the line backwards is where the log scale earns its keep, because the ordinary graph could never show one cell and forty billion on the same page. The line suggests the first cancer cell appeared roughly twelve years before the first scan, which is why early detection matters so much and why doctors think of a tumour's history in doublings.

Look at the last two scans, taken only 32 days apart. Cubing the diameter also cubes its uncertainty, so a 10% slip in measuring D becomes roughly a 30% error in N. That is why the spreadsheet averages three readings for some scans, and why no single point should be trusted to set the slope on its own.

6 The bridge: stretching both axes

The tumour data hides a second kind of relationship. Plot the number of cells against the diameter instead of against time, and you are looking at N = (D/d)3, a power law, in which one quantity is another raised to a fixed power. Stretching only the vertical axis will not straighten this curve, but stretching both axes will.

On a log–log graph a tenfold increase in diameter always climbs exactly three tenfold steps in cell number, so the gradient of the straight line is 3, which is the power in the formula. This is the key idea for the rest of the lesson: if you did not know the power, you could measure it by drawing a log–log graph and reading its gradient.

7 Finding an unknown power in crumpled paper

Squeeze a sheet of paper into a ball and measure its mass and its diameter, then repeat with bigger sheets. A solid ball would follow mass ∝ diameter3 and a flat disc would follow mass ∝ diameter2, so nobody can tell you in advance which power crumpled paper obeys, and the only way to find out is to measure it.

Once both axes are stretched, drag the two gold handles until your line runs through the middle of the points, and read off its gradient. Tap any point to leave it out of the calculator's fit, which lets you test what a single suspicious ball does to the answer.

The worked set gives a power close to 2.4, which lies between the flat sheet's 2 and the solid ball's 3, because a crumpled ball is mostly trapped air and bigger balls trap proportionally more of it. Physicists call a non-whole power like this a fractal dimension.

Now switch to the class set. All nine balls together give a gradient below 1, which would mean a bigger ball is somehow less dense than a flat sheet, and the culprit is ball 4, whose 5 g of paper supposedly measured 1.75 cm on all three attempts. Leave it out and the gradient jumps to about 2.6. The class masses also span less than one power of ten, so small measuring errors tilt the line far more than they do in the worked set, which spans more than two.

8 Two kinds of stretched graph

Semi-log

Log vertical axis, ordinary horizontal axis. A straight line means exponential growth, where the quantity multiplies by the same factor in equal times, and the slope gives the doubling time, as it did for the tumour.

Log–log

Both axes logarithmic. A straight line means a power law, and the gradient of that line is the power itself, which is how we found 3 for cells in a tumour and about 2.4 for paper in a ball.

Multiplying every value by the same constant, whether through a unit change or a bug like the halved diameter, slides a log graph up or down without tilting it, so the doubling time and the power survive even when the spreadsheet does not.
