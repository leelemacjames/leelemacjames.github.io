Spreading, swirling and slope

One geometric idea sits behind the inverse-square laws of gravity, electric charge and radiation, the 1/r law of a current-carrying wire, and the way we read a field from a potential graph. Work through the interactive panels, then answer the questions.

A. A fixed amount spread over a growing shell

Imagine a point source that sends out a fixed number of field lines, or a fixed power of light, in all directions. Every closed shell drawn around the source is crossed by the same total, because nothing is created or destroyed between one shell and the next. The share arriving at each square metre of the shell therefore depends only on how large the shell has become, and a sphere of radius r has area 4πr², whereas a circle of radius r has circumference 2πr.

The sixteen rays always cross every shell, yet the gap between neighbouring rays widens in proportion to r in two dimensions and in proportion to r² in three (the shaded wedge shows one gap growing). Field strength and intensity measure lines per unit of shell, so they shrink in the same proportion.

B. Cutting the flux: the probe box

Place a small imaginary box in a field and ask two questions of it. The first, which we can call the sprinkler question, asks whether more field leaves the box through its faces than enters, so that the box behaves like a source (this is what the word divergence describes). The second, the paddle-wheel question, asks whether the field pushes round the edge of the box in a loop, so that a tiny paddle wheel would turn (this is what curl describes). Drag the box, resize it, and change the field. The numbers use units in which the source strength is 1.

1.0
The point-charge field has a fixed total flux of 4π through any box that contains the charge, whatever its size or position, and zero through any box that does not. That constancy is the flux-cutting statement of the inverse-square law, and it has the same content as the shell picture in section A. For the wire, the same job is done by the circulation around the box.

For all three fields, a box that misses the source gives zero flux and zero circulation, because away from the source the field lines are neither created nor destroyed and none of these fields swirls except the wire. The difference shows up when the box contains the source: the two electric fields then give non-zero flux and zero circulation, while the wire gives zero flux and non-zero circulation.

C. The wire: circles instead of spheres

A long straight wire has no charge to spread, since the wire is neutral and the magnetic field lines never begin or end. The field instead swirls round the wire in closed loops, and what stays fixed from one loop to the next is the sum of B along the loop multiplied by its length, which Ampère's law sets equal to μ₀I for any loop enclosing the current. The loop is a circle of circumference 2πr, so B × 2πr = μ₀I, and B falls as 1/r.

5. Ripples spread across the surface of a pond from a point where a stone fell. The energy in the ripple stays constant as it spreads, and the amplitude of a wave is proportional to the square root of its intensity. Deduce how the amplitude depends on the distance from the stone.[3]

Show model answer

The ripple spreads over a circle of circumference 2πr, so the intensity is proportional to 1/r. The amplitude is proportional to the square root of intensity, which gives an amplitude proportional to 1/√r, and this is why pond ripples fade more slowly than sound or light from a point source, whose amplitude falls as 1/r.

6. Explain, using the idea of field lines crossing a sphere, why a point charge in our three-dimensional space cannot have a field that falls as 1/r³.[3]

Show model answer

The number of field lines leaving the charge is fixed by the size of the charge, and the same number must cross every sphere centred on it. The sphere's area grows as r², so the lines per unit area, which is the field strength, can only fall as 1/r². A 1/r³ field would mean that lines had vanished between one sphere and the next.

7. The magnetic field lines round a straight wire are closed circles. Use the box probe from section B to explain why no net magnetic flux leaves a small box placed beside the wire, and state what the loop calculation gives instead.[3]

Show model answer

Every field line that enters the box also leaves it, since the lines are closed loops with no start or end, so the net flux is zero for any box. What the field does have is a circulation, because adding up B along a loop that encloses the wire gives μ₀I, and that non-zero sum is the swirl that curl describes.

E. Reading the field from the slope of the potential

Every radial field met in this course can also be found from a potential graph. The field in the radial direction is the negative of the gradient of the potential, E = −ΔV/Δr for electricity and g = −ΔV/Δr for gravity, so a steep graph means a strong field and the sign of the slope tells us which way the field points. Move the point along the curve and compare the slope of the tangent with the field calculated from the inverse-square law.

The tangent flattens as r grows, and the slope shrinks as 1/r², which returns us to the inverse-square law from the other side. The electric slope is negative, so the radial field points outward, whereas the gravitational slope is positive, so the field points inward toward the mass.

8. Use the electric setting with r = 0.10 m. Read the potential and calculate the field strength from kQ/r². Then state what value the slope of the tangent should have, including its sign.[3]

Show model answer

V = kQ/r = (8.99 × 10⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹)/0.10 ≈ 450 V, and E = kQ/r² ≈ 4.5 × 10³ V m⁻¹ directed outward. Because E equals minus the gradient, the tangent should have a gradient of about −4.5 × 10³ V m⁻¹, and the panel confirms this.

9 (stretch). The wire's field circulates, so that the sum of B round a full loop is μ₀I rather than zero. Suggest why this field cannot be described by a single potential whose slope gives B, in the way that the electric field of a point charge can.[2]

Show model answer

A field taken from a potential returns to the same value after a round trip, so the sum of the field along any closed loop is zero, and the electric field of a point charge does this. The wire's field gives μ₀I round a loop, so a single-valued potential cannot exist for it, which is the same fact as the field having curl on the wire.
