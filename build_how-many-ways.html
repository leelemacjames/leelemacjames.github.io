A staged problem — statistical mechanics & the arithmetic of hidden arrangements

How Many Ways Can It Look the Same?

A count of everything that could be true and still look exactly like what's visible from outside, built from coins, cubes, and marbles long before a single joule of heat enters the picture.

The usual opening move, calculate the entropy, asks for a number without ever asking what the number is counting. A gentler question turns out to be accessible to a far younger student: how many different hidden arrangements look identical from the outside? Permutations, probability, the logarithm, even heat itself, all fall out of taking that question seriously before reaching for a single formula.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no formula for entropy, handed over in advance
  • no "disorder" metaphor, assumed rather than earned
  • no logarithm, until multiplication has already become inconvenient

An AI can compute a binomial coefficient before the question finishes being typed. It cannot supply the moment a student notices, unprompted, that three of the eight coin outcomes all deserve exactly the same two-word description.

Three separate coin sequences all answer to the single description "two heads." Without being told any vocabulary for it, a distinction has already been drawn between one particular arrangement and the coarser description that several different arrangements can share.

02

Four coloured cubes

Four cubes sit in a row. Each cube is one of exactly two colours, brass or teal, and only the colour sequence matters, not which physical cube occupies which slot. Fixing how many of the four are brass fixes a macrostate; a microstate is one specific left-to-right colouring that achieves it. Two rows with two brass cubes each are different microstates of the very same macrostate if the brass cubes sit in different places along the row.

The machine below fixes a row length, N, and a target number of brass cubes, k, then draws every distinct row that has exactly k brass cubes among its N positions, one row per arrangement. Leave it set to four cubes, two brass, for now, and read the caption above the rows before looking at them.

03

Six students around a table

Six students sit down, four in blue shirts and two in red. Set N to six and k to four on the machine above, brass now standing in for blue, teal for red; the enumeration panel is still just about able to draw every seating pattern that gives the same colour split, though it is noticeably more crowded than four cubes ever was.

04

Marbles in boxes

Set N back to four, representing four identical marbles split between two boxes, and sweep k from 0 to 4. Each value of k is a different macrostate, a different split between the boxes, four-zero, three-one, two-two, one-three, zero-four. Watch the bar chart rather than the number: one of those five splits is unmistakably taller than the rest.

05

Eight particles

Set N to eight. The enumeration panel gives up and reports a bare count instead of a list, which is exactly the honest thing to do once listing stops being a reasonable strategy. The bar chart, notably, has no trouble at all; it never needed to enumerate anything to know the shape of the distribution.

Phase II

Probability, the most likely macrostates

06

Only now, mention probability

Suppose every individual microstate, every fully specified arrangement, is equally likely. Press "Show as probability" on the machine above and the bars barely change shape; they are simply rescaled so the whole set adds up to one. The macrostate with the most microstates was already the tallest bar, and probability turns out to be riding on the back of counting that had already been done.

Phase III

Information, how many questions it would take

07

Introduce Ω

The count that the machine has been computing this whole time has earned a name and a symbol.

Reveal the symbol
Ω(N, k) = N! ⁄ [k!(N−k)!]

A bigger Ω simply means more ways of arranging things that all look the same from outside, which is the entire content of the symbol; nothing further is being claimed by it yet.

08

Why logarithm?

Suppose two independent systems are pushed together: system A with some number of microstates, system B with another. The combined system's microstates multiply, not add, since every arrangement of A can pair with every arrangement of B.

It would be convenient if some measure of "how many ways" simply added when two systems combine, the way lengths add or masses add, rather than multiplying. The two routes below either confirm or deny whether the logarithm delivers exactly that.

the multiplication route

ΩA × ΩB = 1000

ln(ΩA × ΩB) = 6.91

the addition-after-log route

ln ΩA = 3.00

ln ΩB = 3.91

sum = 6.91

The two totals always agree, because a logarithm is exactly the operation that turns multiplying into adding. Once that is in hand, a second and quite different question about the same number becomes worth asking: how many yes-or-no questions would it take to pin down which one of Ω equally likely arrangements is actually the case? Each good question can at best cut the remaining possibilities in half, so after q questions at most 2q arrangements can be told apart.

2q = Ω   ⇒   q = log2Ω

The same logarithm that made two systems add instead of multiply also measures, in bits, exactly how much is still unknown once only the macrostate has been observed.

Phase IV

Thermodynamics, heat opening access to more arrangements

09

Only now, discuss heat

Everything so far has involved no temperature, no energy, and no heat at all, which makes it worth asking, only now, why adding heat to a system should have anything to do with any of it.

Reveal Boltzmann's equation
S = kB ln Ω

Adding heat to a system raises its total energy, and a system with more energy on hand has more ways to share that energy among its particles: more accessible microstates, a larger Ω, and by the equation above, a larger entropy. The equation is no longer a definition to memorise; it is a restatement, in the units physics happens to use for energy and temperature, of the same counting exercise that started with three coins.

The same modelling question that organises every other investigation in this series applies here too: what can be observed directly, and what is only being inferred?

observed

pressure, volume, temperature, total energy, magnetisation: the macrostate

inferred

the enormous number of hidden arrangements compatible with those observations: the microstates

Entropy, counted this way, is the size of everything left unresolved once the observations have been fixed, arrived at through nothing more exotic than coins, cubes, and marbles, all the way up to a joule of heat.
