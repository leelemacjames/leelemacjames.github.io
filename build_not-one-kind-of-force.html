A synthesis — Coulomb, Lorentz, and Ohm compared

Not One Kind of Force

Three investigations, three laws, and a shared habit of notation that quietly asks students to treat all three as the same kind of thing.

Three separate investigations have each produced a law written in roughly the shape "force equals something": Coulomb's law, for two charges at rest; the Lorentz force, for a charge in motion; and Ohm's law, for current moving through a material. Set side by side rather than studied one at a time, they turn out to be three quite different kinds of claim, and the comparison is more revealing than any one of them examined alone.

Even this much is worth pausing on: two of the three depend on where things are, one depends on how fast something is moving, and none of them share a source in quite the same sense.

02

Whether it does work

The three laws also disagree, in an interesting way, about energy. Coulomb's force is conservative: it can be written as minus the gradient of a potential energy, and that potential energy is recovered in full if the charges return to their starting separation.

The Lorentz magnetic force is not merely non-conservative; it is stranger than that. Because the magnetic part of the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, it can never do any work on the particle at all, regardless of the path taken. A charge moving purely under a magnetic field changes direction endlessly without ever changing speed.

The resistive response behind Ohm's law sits at the opposite extreme: it is dissipative, converting ordered electrical energy into heat, irreversibly, with nothing recovered afterward. Conservative, incapable of work, and dissipative are three distinct relationships with energy, not three points on one scale.

03

Interaction law, or constitutive law

The deepest split in the table is the last row. Coulomb's law and the Lorentz force are both interaction laws: statements about how any two charges, anywhere, must relate to one another, built from geometry and a handful of universal constants. Ohm's law is a constitutive law: a statement about how one particular kind of material happens to respond, valid over the range it was measured in and not guaranteed to hold outside it.

interaction law

true of the relationship itself; the same for any two charges anywhere in the universe

constitutive law

true of a material's behaviour; different for copper, for silicon, for salt water

04

More laws to sort

The same question can be asked of almost any named "law" met in a physics course. Try sorting each of the following before reading its answer.

Newton's law of gravitation

interaction law

Any two masses, anywhere, attract according to the same universal constant; nothing about either object's material enters the formula.

Hooke's law, for a spring

constitutive law

The spring constant depends on the particular spring, its material and geometry, and the linear relationship itself only holds for small enough extensions.

Kinetic friction, F = μN

constitutive law

The coefficient μ depends on which two surfaces are in contact, is determined empirically, and is well known to fail as a simple constant outside a limited regime.

Archimedes' principle, buoyant force

interaction law, of a sort

It follows from the universal behaviour of a pressure field in a gravitational field, not from any property of the particular floating object, which puts it closer to the Coulomb side of the line.

05

One symbol, three claims

Why, given how different these four rows turn out to be, are interaction laws and constitutive laws so often written down with exactly the same letter, force equals something, in exactly the same Newton's-second-law grammar?

Part of the answer is convenience: writing everything as a force lets a single method, Newton's second law, be applied uniformly regardless of where the force came from. But the convenience carries a cost. A placeholder symbol used across a fundamental interaction, a velocity-dependent interaction, and an empirical material response quietly invites the belief that all three are the same kind of entity, simply because they are dressed in the same notation, and that belief is exactly the sort of category error worth catching before it hardens into a habit.

An AI can list all four rows of the table above in a single reply. What it cannot do for a student is the harder, slower work of noticing, unprompted, that the letter F was doing three different jobs the whole time.

This is the same distinction, arrived at from three different directions: an interaction law describes a relationship that exists independently of any particular material, and a constitutive law describes how one material happens to respond, and confusing the two is one of the quieter ways a formula can be memorised without ever being understood.
