Making curves straight

Why a billion pounds takes 100 000 years to save and only about two centuries to grow, and how choosing the right axis turns every curve in this story into a straight line you can read.

1 Two series, two kinds of growth

Saving £10 000 a year without interest gives the arithmetic series 10 000, 20 000, 30 000, and so on, so after n years you hold 10 000n pounds and reaching 109 needs n = 109 ÷ 104 = 105. Invest the same payments at a rate r and each year’s balance is multiplied by 1 + r before the next £10 000 arrives, so just after the nth payment the first £10 000 has grown for n − 1 years and the latest for none, and the total is a geometric series whose sum formula divides by the common ratio minus one, 1.04 − 1 = 0.04:

Sn = 10 000 (1 + 1.04 + … + 1.04n−1) = 10 000 (1.04n − 1) ÷ 0.04

Setting this equal to 109 gives 1.04n = 4001, and taking logarithms of both sides turns the unknown power into something we can divide by, n = log 4001 ÷ log 1.04 ≈ 211.5, so the balance first passes a billion after 212 years. Move the rate slider in the panel above and notice that halving the rate does far more than double the wait.

2 Differences tell you which graph will be straight

Choose a sequence and look down the columns. Whichever column settles to a constant, highlighted in gold, tells you which axes will turn the points into a straight line.

Constant first differences mean a straight line on ordinary axes. Constant second differences mean a quadratic, which is straight when plotted against n² rather than n. A constant ratio means a geometric sequence, which is straight once the vertical axis is logarithmic. Saving every year at 4% is a curious mixture, because its first differences are themselves geometric, and for large n it behaves almost exactly like a geometric sequence.

3 Straightening a curve by changing the axis

A quadratic curves on ordinary axes, yet if the horizontal axis is ruled so that equal distances mean equal changes in x² rather than in x, the same points fall on a straight line. The axis labels still show x, but they bunch up on the left and spread out on the right, exactly as the labels on log paper do. A square-root law straightens in the opposite way, on an axis ruled by √x.

This is linearisation in its plainest form: write the law as Y = mX + c by choosing X and Y cleverly, plot Y against X, and read the constants from the gradient and the intercept. For the pendulum, T = 2π√(L/g) becomes a line through the origin when T is plotted against √L, and its gradient, 2π/√g, gives a measurement of g.

4 Semi-log graphs: exponential growth becomes a line

For a model y = abx, taking logarithms gives log y = log a + x log b, which is a straight line when log y is plotted against x. A logarithmic vertical axis does this for you, because each gridline is ten times the one below. The same machinery describes compound interest and the growth of a tumour, so switch between them.

On ordinary axes the mattress saver never leaves the floor of the money graph, while on the logarithmic axis the same straight line bends over, because adding £10 000 to a growing pile is a smaller and smaller multiplication. Straightness depends on the axes as much as on the data.

5 Log–log graphs: power laws become a line

For a model y = axn, taking logarithms gives log y = log a + n log x, so plotting log y against log x gives a straight line whose gradient is the power n and whose intercept is log a. Both axes must be stretched this time.

The tumour gives a power of exactly 3, because the number of cells is the volume ratio (D/d)3. Crumpled paper gives about 2.4, between a flat sheet’s 2 and a solid ball’s 3, because a crumpled ball is mostly trapped air; nobody could have told you that power in advance, and the log–log gradient is how you measure it.
