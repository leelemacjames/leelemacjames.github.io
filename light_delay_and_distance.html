Light, Delay and Distance

Rømer turned a delay into a speed, and the astronomers who followed him turned speeds, angles and brightnesses into distances, so that observational cosmology can be read as a long sequence of comparisons between something known and something seen. This page runs the first of those comparisons with you in charge of the telescope, extends it to the recession of the galaxies, and then lets an error enter at the bottom of the distance ladder and travel upward.

Part one

IIo, the Earth and the eleven-minute drift

Jupiter’s innermost large moon slips into the planet’s shadow once every 1.769 days, and an observer on Earth records the moment it vanishes. With the speed of light set to infinite the recorded times keep perfect step with Io’s orbit, and with a finite speed the eclipses arrive early while the Earth approaches Jupiter and late while it recedes, so that the dots on the right trace out Rømer’s curve as the year goes round. Drag the Earth around its orbit, change the speed of light, and add timing noise to see how much of the signal survives.

Predict first

When the Earth is nearest to Jupiter, will eclipses be recorded earlier or later than the average, and by about how many seconds does a single orbit of Io drift out of step?

Show the answer

Earlier. The light-travel time between the nearest and farthest positions differs by the time light needs to cross the Earth’s orbit, about 998 seconds or 16.6 minutes, so the curve swings roughly 8.3 minutes either side of its average. Spread over the 112 or so orbits of Io in half a year this is about 9 seconds per orbit, which is invisible in any one eclipse and unmistakable once the months have added them up. Rømer’s own estimate of the swing was larger, 22 minutes in total, and the difference is the subject of the estimator below.

Part two

IIThe galaxies run away in proportion

Hubble’s graph of recession speed against distance is a straight line through the origin, and its slope H₀, in kilometres per second for every megaparsec, turns any distance into a speed. Drag the yellow galaxy outward and its arrow lengthens in step, then switch to the yellow galaxy’s own point of view, where the Milky Way is the one running away and the law looks exactly the same.

Predict first

If the yellow galaxy is moved to twice its present distance, what happens to its speed, and what would an astronomer in that galaxy measure for the Milky Way?

Show the answer

The speed doubles, since it is proportional to distance, and the astronomer would find the Milky Way receding at the speed that Hubble’s law gives for the separation between the two galaxies, with every other galaxy receding in the same proportion. A law of this form has no preferred centre, which is why the expansion cannot be located at any one galaxy.

Part three

IIIA ladder in which every rung leans on the one below

The film in which Terence Tao and Grant Sanderson walk up the cosmic distance ladder moves through Venus, the speed of light, nearby stars, the Milky Way, nearby galaxies and distant galaxies, and each of those stages is calibrated by the one beneath it. A systematic error in any calibration therefore rides upward through every later rung, and the sliders below let you place one wherever you like and read off what it does to a distance and to the Hubble constant that is built from it.

Part four

IVReflecting on distance in general

Every distance quoted beyond the surface of the Earth is a delay in disguise: the astronomical unit is 499 seconds of light travel, a light-year is a year of it, and the radar echo that fixes the scale of the inner solar system is a stopwatch reading multiplied by a speed that has been exact since 1983. Rømer’s contribution was to see that a delay can be read in either direction, so that a known distance yields a speed, and the redefinition of the metre completed the loop by fixing the speed and deriving the lengths from it.

The Earth’s orbit does double duty in this story, because its diameter was the extra path that delayed Io’s eclipses and the same diameter is the baseline from which the parallax of the nearest stars is measured, so that one number sits beneath both Rømer’s speed of light and the first rung that leaves the solar system. An error there travels upward intact, which is why the 6% shortfall in the orbit size of the 1670s would, in the ladder above, shift every later distance by the same 6% and the inferred expansion rate by about 7% in the opposite direction.

Measurements also differ in what they can conceal. A random error shrinks as observations accumulate, as the scatter of the dots on the eclipse plot shows when you log a second year, whereas a systematic error such as a mis-scaled orbit survives any amount of averaging and can be exposed only by an independent method, which is how the disagreements between Fizeau and Foucault and the later agreement around Michelson taught physicists where to place their trust.

Constants differ as well. The speed of light stopped being measured when the metre was defined through it, and the astronomical unit was fixed as an exact number of metres in 2012, so the lowest rungs of the ladder are now exact by convention and the uncertainty lives higher up in the calibration of standard candles, while Newton’s constant, which Cavendish weighed in a shed, has no convention to shelter behind. The expansion rate shows the same point from the top: estimates of H₀ from the distance ladder and from the early universe have disagreed by several per cent in recent years, the so-called Hubble tension, and the current state of that disagreement is worth checking in the literature, since asking which assumption each rung rests on is worth more than any single value.

Rømer’s delay curve repeats every year, yet Io’s true period never changed. What would the curve look like if the period were slowly lengthening, and how could you tell the two effects apart?

A lengthening period would add a drift that grows from year to year, roughly a parabola in the observed-minus-calculated time, whereas the light delay repeats with the geometry of the Earth and Jupiter. Only observations spread over many years can separate the two, which is exactly what the long Paris programme supplied.

Switch on “Let Jupiter move” and log two years. Why do the two years differ, and what does that say about assuming a fixed Jupiter?

The Earth and Jupiter return to the same relative positions every 399 days and not every 365, so the second year’s curve is shifted in phase from the first. Treating Jupiter as fixed is a good first approximation for half a year and a poorer one over several, and it is one of the reasons that Rømer’s extrapolated delay was inexact.

A ladder overestimates every distance by 8%. How should the Hubble constant derived from it be corrected?

The true distances are smaller by that factor, so the same speeds divided by smaller distances give an H₀ about 8% higher than the one quoted.

Why is comparing a galaxy’s recession speed with c meaningful only for small redshifts?

The simple relation v = cz comes from the Doppler shift at low speed, and at large redshift the stretching of space itself and the choice of distance definition both matter. A naive speed above c at great distance involves no signal travelling faster than light, since it describes the growth of the separation and not motion through space.

Name one measurement from your own teaching in which a systematic error would survive averaging, and say how you would expose it.

Any answer here is a good one; the useful habit is to ask for an independent method that fails in a different way, as Foucault’s mirror did for Fizeau’s wheel.
