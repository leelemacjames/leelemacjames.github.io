Energy for escape, field for orbit, and moving between them

The hard IB questions about rockets and satellites are all solved by choosing the right one of two tools, and by knowing how they meet in the single result E = −GMm/2r. Every panel below uses the Earth, with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m, and a spacecraft of mass 1000 kg.

Which tool, and why

The field strength of a point mass falls as 1/r², so the force on a rocket changes all the way up, and the work done against it is an integral that we would otherwise have to evaluate at every stage. The gravitational potential V = −GM/r is that integral already done, with the zero of energy placed at infinity, so the work needed to move a mass m between two points is simply mΔV, whatever path is taken. This is why any question that compares two positions, such as escape, a maximum height, or a change of orbit, is answered fastest with energy.

A circular orbit asks a different question, because the speed of a satellite is fixed by the requirement that the gravitational force supplies exactly the centripetal force, mv²/r = GMm/r², and that balance holds at a single radius at a time. Energy on its own cannot say how fast the satellite must move at a given radius, so the field strength g = GM/r² is the tool there. The two tools meet in the total energy of the orbit, which comes out as a neat negative number once the orbital speed from the force balance is fed into the energy expression.

A. Moving between equipotentials

Two shells of constant potential are shown around the Earth. The work done by the rocket engines to move a mass between them, slowly enough that no kinetic energy is gained, is mΔV, which is the vertical gap between the two points on the potential graph. The familiar shortcut mgh treats the field as constant and therefore follows the tangent to the curve rather than the curve itself.

The curve bends away from its tangent, so the constant-g estimate lies above the true change in potential whenever the rocket climbs a large fraction of the radius. For small heights the tangent and the curve are indistinguishable, which is why mgh works near the surface and fails for a climb to a distant orbit.

B. Escape by energy

A rocket launched straight up at speed v has a total energy per kilogram of ½v² − GM/R, and this total stays constant as it rises (air resistance and engine thrust after launch are ignored). The rocket climbs until all its kinetic energy has been converted, which happens where the total energy line meets the potential curve. If the total energy is zero or positive the line never meets the curve and the rocket escapes, so the escape speed follows from ½v² = GM/R.

The shaded region is the kinetic energy available at each radius, and it shrinks to nothing at the turning point. Because the potential curve flattens toward zero at large distance, even a small positive total energy carries the rocket out to infinity with speed to spare.

C. Orbit by field strength

A satellite on a circular path needs a net inward force, and gravity is the only force acting, so mv²/r = GMm/r² and v = √(GM/r). Inserting this into the energy gives a kinetic energy of GMm/2r, a potential energy of −GMm/r, and a total of −GMm/2r, so the total energy equals minus the kinetic energy and half the potential energy. Move the slider to see the three quantities change, and watch how the period grows with radius.

The animation runs about 1400 times faster than real time, so the low orbit takes roughly four seconds per lap and the geostationary orbit about a minute. The satellite always falls toward the Earth, and the sideways speed carries it past the surface as it falls, which is what circular orbit means.

D. Changing orbit

The energy of a circular orbit is −GMm/2r, so moving to a larger orbit raises the total energy, and the work done by the engines equals that change. The kinetic energy falls by the same amount, because it equals minus the total, and the potential energy rises by twice that amount. A rocket therefore fires its engines to go up and ends with a slower satellite. The standard practical route is the Hohmann transfer, which uses one burn to enter an ellipse that touches both circles, and a second burn at the far end to circularise.

On the energy bars the amber marker is the total energy, and it always sits at the level of the kinetic bar reversed in sign. Watch the two bars as you raise the target orbit, because the kinetic bar shrinks while the potential bar grows, and the total energy climbs toward zero, the level of escape.

Questions

1. A probe of mass 1000 kg is raised from the surface of the Earth to a height equal to the radius of the Earth. Calculate the work done (a) using mgh with g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹ and (b) using gravitational potential, and explain which is valid.[4]

Show model answer

(a) mgh = 1000 × 9.81 × 6.37 × 10⁶ = 6.25 × 10¹⁰ J. (b) V at the surface is −GM/R = −62.5 MJ kg⁻¹ and V at 2R is −31.3 MJ kg⁻¹, so the work is 1000 × 31.3 × 10⁶ = 3.13 × 10¹⁰ J. Only (b) is valid, because the field falls to a quarter of its surface value at 2R, and the constant-g estimate overstates the work by a factor of 2 here.

2. Derive the escape speed from the surface of a planet of mass M and radius R, and explain why an energy argument is preferable to integrating the force.[3]

Show model answer

To escape, the total energy must reach zero at infinity, so ½mv² − GMm/R = 0 and v = √(2GM/R), which is 11.2 km s⁻¹ for the Earth. The force changes with r throughout the climb, so a force argument requires an integral of GMm/r² from R to infinity, and the potential energy −GMm/r is the result of that integral, allowing the answer to be written from the start and end states alone.

3. A rocket is fired vertically from the surface at an initial speed of 8.0 km s⁻¹ and then coasts. Ignoring air resistance and the rotation of the Earth, calculate the greatest distance from the centre of the Earth that it reaches.[4]

Show model answer

The total energy per kilogram is ½(8000)² − 3.982 × 10¹⁴/6.37 × 10⁶ = 3.20 × 10⁷ − 6.25 × 10⁷ = −3.05 × 10⁷ J kg⁻¹. At the highest point the kinetic energy is zero, so −GM/r = −3.05 × 10⁷ and r = 1.31 × 10⁷ m, which is 2.05 Earth radii, or a height of about 6.7 × 10⁶ m above the surface.

4. Use the gravitational field strength to show that the orbital speed of a satellite is v = √(GM/r), and hence show that its total energy is −GMm/2r.[4]

Show model answer

Gravity supplies the centripetal force, so mv²/r = GMm/r², giving v² = GM/r. The kinetic energy is ½mv² = GMm/2r and the potential energy is −GMm/r, so the total is GMm/2r − GMm/r = −GMm/2r. The total is therefore minus the kinetic energy and half the potential energy, and it is negative for every bound orbit.

5. A satellite of mass 1000 kg moves from a circular orbit of radius 6.77 × 10⁶ m to a circular orbit of radius 4.22 × 10⁷ m. Calculate the change in (a) total energy, (b) kinetic energy and (c) potential energy, and explain why the engines must do work on the satellite even though its speed decreases.[6]

Show model answer

The total energies are −GMm/2r, which gives −29.4 GJ for the inner orbit and −4.72 GJ for the outer, so ΔE = +24.7 GJ. The kinetic energy falls from 29.4 GJ to 4.72 GJ, so ΔKE = −24.7 GJ, and the potential energy rises from −58.8 GJ to −9.44 GJ, so ΔPE = +49.4 GJ. The speed drops from 7.67 km s⁻¹ to 3.07 km s⁻¹, but the potential energy gain is twice the kinetic energy loss, and the engines must supply the net 24.7 GJ.

6. Air resistance causes a satellite to spiral down from an orbit of altitude 400 km to an orbit of altitude 300 km. State and explain what happens to its speed, its kinetic energy, its potential energy and its total energy.[5]

Show model answer

The speed rises from 7.669 km s⁻¹ to 7.727 km s⁻¹, because v = √(GM/r) grows as r shrinks. For each 1000 kg the kinetic energy rises by 0.44 GJ, the potential energy falls by 0.88 GJ, and the total energy falls by 0.44 GJ. Drag does negative work and removes energy from the orbit, while the fall in the gravitational field releases twice as much potential energy as drag removes, and half of that surplus appears as extra kinetic energy.

7. A satellite in a circular orbit of radius 6.77 × 10⁶ m has a mass of 1000 kg. Calculate the additional kinetic energy, and the additional speed, that it needs in order to escape from the Earth, and compare the energy with that needed to escape from the surface.[4]

Show model answer

The total energy is −29.4 GJ, so 29.4 GJ more must be supplied to reach zero, and this equals the present kinetic energy. The escape speed at this radius is √2 times the orbital speed, which is 10.8 km s⁻¹, so the extra speed is 3.18 km s⁻¹. Escaping from the surface needs 62.5 GJ for the same mass, so a satellite already in low orbit has done about half of the work.

8 (stretch). For the Hohmann transfer from the 400 km orbit to the geostationary orbit, the transfer ellipse has a semi-major axis a = (r₁ + r₂)/2, and the speed at any point on it obeys v² = GM(2/r − 1/a). Calculate the two burns and the time taken for the transfer.[5]

Show model answer

With r₁ = 6.77 × 10⁶ m and r₂ = 4.22 × 10⁷ m, a = 2.45 × 10⁷ m. The speed at the low point of the ellipse is 10.07 km s⁻¹ against a circular speed of 7.67 km s⁻¹, so the first burn is 2.40 km s⁻¹. At the far point the ellipse speed is 1.62 km s⁻¹ against a circular speed of 3.07 km s⁻¹, so the second burn is 1.46 km s⁻¹. The transfer takes half an orbital period of the ellipse, π√(a³/GM), which is about 5.3 hours.
