A staged problem — electrostatics & power-law reasoning

How Does Repulsion Fade?

Two charged balloons, an imperfect apparatus, and a search for the shape of a law rather than a confirmation of the one everybody already knows.

Two charged balloons repel one another, and the question staged below is not whether an inverse-square law holds, but what kind of relationship the apparatus in front of the class actually supports, given that charge, geometry, and force are all, in practice, imperfectly controlled.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no assumed exponent
  • no Coulomb's law asserted in advance
  • no promise that a balloon behaves like a point charge

An AI can state Coulomb's law in a single sentence, and stating it settles nothing that matters here. What follows treats the exponent as something to be earned from noisy, imperfect measurements, in full view of the reasons a real apparatus might mislead.

01

Two ways to see it

The same interaction can be read off an apparatus in two different ways, and comparing them is itself informative. Suspend two similarly charged balloons from a rod by threads, and they come to rest deflected outward, held there once the horizontal electric force is balanced by a component of each thread's tension; resolving those forces along and across the thread gives a direct route from a measured angle to a force.

T sinθ = F     T cosθ = mg   ⇒   F = mg tanθ

This converts a visible deflection into a force estimate, but it also raises a geometrical question the point-charge idealisation usually hides: what exactly counts as the separation, r, between two objects that plainly have size? Several answers are all defensible at once, and the disagreement between them is worth sitting with rather than resolving too quickly.

  • centre to centre
  • surface to surface
  • nearest charged regions
  • separation before the balloons settle, or after

A charged object can instead be arranged so the electric force acts vertically, and read directly from the change in a balance reading.

F = Δm · g

The vertical arrangement gives a more direct force reading, while the horizontal arrangement makes the geometry more visible; agreement between the two would be real evidence that both are measuring the same thing.

02

First measurements

Keeping the charge on each balloon as nearly fixed as practical, measure the force at several separations and build a table exactly as a real apparatus would force you to. The first question worth asking is entirely qualitative: when the separation doubles, does the force drop to roughly a half, a quarter, an eighth, or something else?

F ∝ 1⁄r F ∝ 1⁄r² F ∝ 1⁄r³

03

Doubling and constant-product tests

Write the general candidate model as a constant divided by the separation raised to some power, where the exponent describes how rapidly the interaction weakens and the constant absorbs the charge strengths and everything else that stays fixed.

F(r) = A ⁄ rn

Doubling the separation gives a direct test, accessible before any logarithm is needed: if the model is right, doubling r should divide the force by exactly two raised to the power n.

F(r) ⁄ F(2r) = 2n

Record two measurements with separations roughly a factor of two apart to use this test.

A second test asks, for a handful of candidate exponents, whether r to that power times F stays roughly constant across every recorded measurement. Whichever candidate keeps the product closest to constant is the best-supported model so far.

Pn(r) = rn F(r)   — nearly constant if n is close to correct

04

Logarithmic linearisation

Later, the same relationship can be rewritten by taking logarithms of both sides, turning a power law into a straight line whose gradient is exactly minus n.

log F = log A − n log r

Plotted this way, the logarithm stops being a graphing ritual and becomes a device for pulling the exponent out of the data, worth reaching for only once the two more transparent tests above have already built some trust in the method.

05

The crucial complication

This is where the problem becomes richer than a routine confirm-the-law practical: a balloon is nothing like a mathematical point charge.

A balloon is
  • large relative to the separation
  • deformable
  • electrically polarisable
  • unevenly charged
  • prone to charge leakage
  • affected by humidity
  • moved by air currents
  • capable of redistributing charge as another balloon approaches

A poor fit to an inverse-square law does not, on its own, refute electrostatics; it may simply reveal where the point-charge idealisation stops being defensible. A plausible criterion for when it becomes safe again is that the separation should exceed the balloon's own radius by a comfortable margin.

r >rsim few × R    (R = balloon radius)

Rather than treating that as a fixed rule, it is worth investigating how the apparent exponent drifts as the range of separations used in the fit changes, which gives a small hierarchy of models rather than one verdict: close in, the data are messy and no clean power law fits well; at intermediate separations something close to a power law appears, though the fitted exponent can still drift with the range chosen; far out, the exponent settles down and stays put.

06

From force to potential

Once an empirical force law has been proposed, it is worth asking what potential-energy landscape would generate it. Force and potential energy are related by the force being minus the gradient of the potential.

F = −dU⁄dr

Integrating a repulsive force of the general power-law form gives a potential energy that falls off one power more gently than the force itself.

F(r) = k ⁄ rn   ⇒   U(r) = k ⁄ [(n−1) rn−1]

For the inverse-square case in particular, choosing the constant of integration so the potential vanishes at infinite separation gives the familiar result.

n = 2   ⇒   U(r) = k ⁄ r

Force and potential energy are often introduced in the same breath, which blurs a distinction worth keeping sharp: they do not fall off at the same rate, and the difference between them is exactly one power of r.

07

A productive sequence of questions

Not every stage above needs to be worked in order for the thinking to be genuine; the questions themselves are the more durable output.

  1. What evidence shows that the interaction weakens with distance?
  2. How can a deflection or a balance reading be converted into a force?
  3. What counts as the separation between two balloons?
  4. Which simple power law best describes the measurements?
  5. Does the inferred exponent depend on the range of distances used?
  6. Is the charge actually constant during the experiment?
  7. When does a balloon behave sufficiently like a point charge?
  8. What potential-energy function corresponds to the measured force law?
  9. Why might the potential decay more slowly than the force?
  10. What further observation would distinguish experimental error from model failure?
08

Why inverse square

Only after an empirical model has been built from actual measurements is it worth asking why the exponent might land on two in particular. It is worth resisting this section until that point.

Reveal the geometrical argument

Influence from a point source spreads through three-dimensional space across spherical surfaces, and the area of a sphere grows with the square of its radius.

A(r) = 4πr²

If the same total flux passes through every one of those spheres, the flux crossing a unit of area must fall as the reciprocal of that area.

flux density ∝ 1⁄r²

That motivates an inverse-square field; it does not, by itself, prove Coulomb's law, because the argument leans on assumptions of its own.

  • three-dimensional Euclidean space
  • spherical symmetry
  • conserved flux
  • no screening or intervening sources

The inverse-square law turns out to be a geometrical model with stated assumptions, not an isolated formula pulled from nowhere.

The classroom formulation

Task sheet

How does repulsion fade?

Two charged balloons repel one another. Construct the strongest model you can for how the interaction depends on separation.

You may use suspended balloons, balances, geometrical measurements, candidate power laws, graphs, dimensional reasoning, and potential energy.

You are not expected to obtain an exact inverse-square law. Your task is to decide:

  • what the apparatus genuinely shows;
  • which model is best supported;
  • where the model ceases to be credible;
  • what additional assumptions are needed to connect the measurements to point-charge electrostatics.

An AI can state Coulomb's law immediately. It cannot replace the work of deciding what was measured, what was inferred, what idealisations were used, and what the deviations actually mean.
