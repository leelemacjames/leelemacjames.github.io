A staged problem — optics & variational reasoning

The Fastest Crossing

Two transparent materials, a boundary between them, and a law kept out of sight until the evidence has already built its own case for it.

Light travels from a point A in one transparent material to a point B in another, and for now the two materials are known only to transmit light at different speeds. The question is how light should choose its crossing point on the boundary between them, if the journey is to take the least possible time.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no normal
  • no angle of incidence
  • no Snell's law
  • no refractive index

Each of those is a discovery still to be motivated, not a tool to be handed over in advance. The goal of the ten stages below is not to arrive at a symbol; it is to build, from nothing but distance and time, the idea that light seems to care about time rather than distance at all.

01

Build a time model

In each material light travels in a straight line, so once a crossing point is chosen, the two legs of the journey are ordinary straight-line distances, and time is simply distance over speed in each material separately. If the crossing point sits a horizontal distance x from directly beneath A, this is already a genuine optimisation problem, with one free variable and a function to minimise.

T(x) = √(a² + x²) ⁄ v1 + √(b² + (d−x)²) ⁄ v2

Nothing here has been assumed about the answer. A, B, and the two speeds are given; everything else follows from writing down what time actually costs.

02

Try different crossing points

Pick perhaps ten crossing points along the boundary, and for each one work out the distance travelled in material 1, the distance travelled in material 2, and the total time. Done by hand or in a spreadsheet, a pattern appears quickly: there is one best crossing point, flanked on both sides by worse ones.

03

Ask what matters

Now push on the slowness slider above and watch the best crossing point move. As medium 2 is made slower, the best path spends less distance inside it, and the crossing point drifts back toward B's own side of the boundary; light behaves as though it prefers to remain in the fast medium for as long as it reasonably can. That is already a physical intuition, invented before any law was written down.

04

Invent a property

Speed has been doing a lot of work in the phrase "slow medium" and "fast medium", but it is worth asking whether a single quantity could describe how reluctant a medium is to transmit light in the first place. Candidates worth trying out loud include resistance, delay, drag, cost, or slowness; the last of these is simplest, and is defined as the reciprocal of speed.

s = 1 ⁄ v

Rewriting the time model in terms of slowness rather than speed changes nothing about the physics, but it changes what the medium is: it no longer has a speed at all, only a time cost per metre.

T(x) = s1√(a² + x²) + s2√(b² + (d−x)²)
05

Geometry appears

Sooner or later, describing how steep each leg of the path is requires a consistent way to talk about angle, and there are two equally reasonable places to measure it from: relative to the boundary itself, or relative to the line perpendicular to it, the normal. Only now is it worth introducing either.

measured from the boundary

measured from the normal

The normal wins out for reasons that only become obvious once both are drawn: a ray travelling straight through, undeflected, gets a clean angle of zero from the normal, while from the boundary it would awkwardly be ninety degrees; the normal also makes reflection symmetric in a way the boundary angle does not; and the perpendicular distances used back in stage one, a and b, are themselves already measured from the boundary along the normal direction. The normal turns out to be motivated, not arbitrary.

07

Only now, reveal the law

By this point the class has a table of predictions, an intuition about slowness, and a motivated definition of angle. The only question left is whether some single geometric quantity stays constant across the boundary no matter where the crossing point sits, and it is worth letting that question sit for a while before opening the derivation below.

Reveal the law
setting the slope of T to zero
s1 · x⁄√(a²+x²) − s2 · (d−x)⁄√(b²+(d−x)²) = 0
recognising the two ratios as angles from the normal
x⁄√(a²+x²) = sinθ1     (d−x)⁄√(b²+(d−x)²) = sinθ2
the compact summary
s1 sinθ1 = s2 sinθ2      equivalently      sinθ1⁄v1 = sinθ2⁄v2

This is the law usually met the other way round, handed over before any of the reasoning that makes it inevitable. Here it is the endpoint of six stages of evidence, rather than the starting line.

The natural next question is whether nature might be quietly solving optimisation problems like this one everywhere, which is close to the full statement of Fermat's Principle. The same seed grows into a family of related ideas, each one asking a version of the same question in a different setting.

Where n comes from

Framing a medium by its slowness rather than its speed gives every path a simple cost: multiply the slowness of each stretch of material by the distance covered in it, and add.

C = Σi si Δsi      C = ∫ s ds

This is exactly the logic of walking across uneven terrain, where every metre carries its own time cost depending on the ground underfoot; students building this idea are, without the name yet attached, constructing an optical metric well ahead of meeting refractive index or the calculus of variations formally.

The refractive index, when it does arrive, turns out to be nothing more exotic than a normalised slowness: a medium's time cost per metre, measured against the time cost of empty space.

n = s ⁄ svacuum = c ⁄ v

The familiar symbol turns out to be the less fundamental idea; time per unit distance was there first.
