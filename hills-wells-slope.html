Hills, wells and the slope that makes a field

A potential graph turns a field into a landscape. Around a positive charge the landscape is a hill, near a negative charge or a mass it is a well, and the strength of the field at any point is the steepness of the ground beneath it.

The contour map

A contour is a line of equal potential, drawn here at equal steps ΔV, in the way a hillwalker's map draws lines of equal height. Where the contours crowd together the potential changes quickly with distance, which means a steep slope and a strong field, and where they spread apart the ground is gentle and the field weak. The field itself points downhill and crosses every contour at a right angle. Drag the probe anywhere on the map, and choose a landscape below. Units are arbitrary, with k = 1, so a source of strength q gives V = q/r.

The short white bar through the probe lies along the steepest direction and spans the two contours either side of it, so its length is the contour spacing Δr at that point. The measured ratio ΔV/Δr is the average slope across the bar, and it agrees with the exact gradient whenever the bar is short, which is why reading the spacing of contours is a valid way to read the field.

The slope and the relief

The left panel slices the landscape along the steepest line through the probe, so that its tangent has a gradient equal to the field strength at that point. The dotted horizontal lines mark the two contours either side of the probe, and the rise between them divided by the run is the measured slope. The right panel shows the whole landscape as a relief in which hills stand up and wells sink, with the probe resting on the surface (heights are clipped at ±4).

Along the slice the tangent gets steeper as the probe approaches the source and flatter as it moves away, and the gradient changes as 1/r² for a point source. The sign convention makes the field the negative of the gradient, so the field points down the slope, away from a hill and into a well. Gravity gives wells only, since mass has one sign, while electricity gives hills and wells.

Questions

1. On the single positive charge, contours are drawn at equal steps of V. Explain why the spacing between neighbouring contours grows as you move away from the charge, and predict by what factor the spacing at r = 2 exceeds the spacing at r = 1.[3]

Show model answer

The field falls as 1/r², so the slope of the potential flattens with distance, and a fixed step ΔV then needs a longer run Δr = ΔV/E. The spacing is proportional to 1/E and therefore to r², so at r = 2 it is four times the spacing at r = 1.

2. On the dipole, explain why field lines cross the V = 0 contour at right angles, and say where along the line joining the charges the contours are most closely crowded.[3]

Show model answer

Moving along a contour leaves V unchanged, so the field has no component along a contour, and the field must point perpendicular to it. The contours crowd most closely just beside each charge, where the potential changes fastest with distance and the field is strongest.

3. Place the probe on the point midway between the two positive charges. Describe what happens to the contour spacing bar and to the field arrow, and state the value of the field there.[3]

Show model answer

The two fields have equal size and opposite direction at the midpoint, so the field is zero, the arrow shrinks to nothing and the spacing bar cannot be measured because the ground is level in every direction at that instant. The point is a saddle, which is a hill along the line perpendicular to the charges and a valley along the line joining them, and contours cross there in an X.

4. The two unequal masses sit on the horizontal axis, one of strength 1 at x = −1.6 and one of strength 0.4 at x = 2.0. Use the probe to locate the point on the axis where the field is zero, and explain why it lies closer to the smaller mass.[3]

Show model answer

The field is zero at about x = 0.6, where the two pulls cancel. Setting 1/(x + 1.6)² equal to 0.4/(2.0 − x)² gives (2.0 − x)/(x + 1.6) = √0.4 ≈ 0.63, which puts the point 1.4 from the small mass and 2.2 from the large one. The larger mass pulls more strongly at any given distance, so the pulls balance only nearer the weaker source.

5. A map of a region near a charge has equipotentials drawn every 20 V. At point A the neighbouring lines are 0.40 cm apart and at point B they are 1.60 cm apart. Calculate the field strength at each point and compare them.[3]

Show model answer

E ≈ ΔV/Δr, so at A the field is 20/0.0040 = 5000 V m⁻¹ and at B it is 20/0.0160 = 1250 V m⁻¹. The field at A is four times that at B, which is the ratio of the spacings inverted, and both fields point from higher to lower potential.
