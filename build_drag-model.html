A staged problem — fluid resistance & the limits of a single exponent

Is Drag One Law, or Many?

A falling object speeds up until, eventually, it stops speeding up, and the law behind how it got there turns out to depend on how fast it was already going.

Drop something and it accelerates, faster and faster, until at some point it simply stops accelerating and falls the rest of the way at a constant speed. Something must be pushing back exactly as hard as gravity is pulling down, and the natural next question is how that push depends on how fast the object is already moving.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no assumed exponent for the speed dependence
  • no promise that the same law applies at every speed

An AI can quote both the linear and the quadratic drag formula correctly without blinking. It cannot tell you, for this particular object at this particular speed, which one is actually the right description, because that is a question about the world, not about the formula.

01

Something pushes back

The moment an object stops accelerating, Newton's second law says the forces on it must be balanced. For something falling, that means whatever is resisting the motion has grown to exactly match its weight.

mg = D(vt)

D is left deliberately unspecified here: some function of speed that starts at zero when the object is at rest and grows as it speeds up, catching gravity exactly at the terminal speed, vt.

02

Two candidate laws

Two very different functions are both defensible starting guesses for D, and both appear, under different names, in different corners of physics.

D(v) = k₁v D(v) = k₂v²

The first grows in direct proportion to speed, the kind of resistance associated with a thick, syrupy fluid dragging smoothly against a slow-moving object. The second grows with the square of speed, the kind associated with shoving air out of the way fast enough that its own inertia matters. Nothing said so far picks between them.

03

Testing by weight, not by speed

Speed cannot easily be dialled in directly here, since it is the very thing the falling object settles on by itself. Weight can be controlled directly, though, simply by stacking more of the same object together, and the balance condition turns that into an indirect but genuine test.

Writing the candidate law generally as D(v) = kvn and solving the balance condition for the terminal speed gives a clean prediction for how vt should scale with mass.

mg = kvtn   ⇒   vt ∝ m1⁄n

doubling the weight should double the terminal speed

if n = 2

doubling the weight should scale terminal speed by only √2, about 1.41×

05

The regime that decides

Recorded drops rarely sit cleanly at n = 1 or n = 2; the implied exponent tends to drift somewhere between them, and drift further as heavier or lighter stacks are tried. That drift is not measurement error to be apologised for. It is the honest answer: which candidate dominates depends on how fast the object is actually moving relative to the fluid, a balance usually summarised by a single dimensionless number.

Reveal the dimensionless number

The Reynolds number compares how much the fluid's own inertia matters against how much its internal friction, its viscosity, matters, for an object of a given size moving at a given speed.

Re = ρvL ⁄ η

Very roughly, and depending on the object's shape, small Re favours the linear candidate, large Re favours the quadratic one, and the region in between is where neither fits especially well. A dust mote settling through still air, or a marble sinking through syrup, sits at small Re. A dropped stone, a falling raindrop, or a stack of coffee filters in an ordinary classroom mostly sits at large Re, which is why the quadratic law is usually the more useful default for everyday falling objects, without ever being the only correct answer.

06

Not one law, but a family

The two candidates from stage two are not really competitors with one true winner; they are the two extremes of a single underlying resistance that behaves like each of them in its own regime.

D(v) = k₁v + k₂v²

At low speed the first term wins and the object behaves as though drag were linear; at high speed the second term wins and it behaves as though drag were quadratic; in between, neither pure power law is quite right, and that is exactly the regime the gauge on the rig above was tracking the whole time.

quantitykind of law
Coulomb, Lorentzinteraction law, fixed exponent
dragconstitutive law, exponent set by regime

Drag depends on velocity, which puts it, by dependence alone, in the same family as the Lorentz force; but it is a constitutive law, the medium and the object's shape are doing the work, not a universal constant, which puts it much closer in spirit to Ohm's law. The two organising questions, what does it depend on, and what kind of claim is it, do not always point the same way, and drag is the clearest case yet of that mismatch.
