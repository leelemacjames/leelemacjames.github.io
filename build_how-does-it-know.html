A staged problem — thermal equilibrium & the return of counting

How Does It Know Where to Stop?

Two temperatures, one insulated box, and a stopping point that turns out to be a matter of overwhelming likelihood rather than any law pushing it there.

A hot copper ball and cold water, sealed together, end up at the same temperature; that much is familiar to the point of being unremarkable. The question worth staying with is how the system knows to stop exactly there, and whether "knows" is even the right word for something that, as this page tries to show, involves no pushing at all.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no claim that equilibrium simply means equal temperature
  • no formula for the equilibrium temperature, handed over early
  • no assumption that anything pushes energy toward balance

An AI can state that two objects in thermal contact end up at the same temperature without needing to think about it. It cannot supply the moment a student notices that nothing in the argument below ever pushes anything anywhere at all.

Building on

This page leans on the counting machine built for How Many Ways Can It Look the Same?, and reuses its central idea, Ω as a plain count of hidden arrangements, without repeating the earlier stages that earned it.

01

Sketch it before you calculate it

A copper ball at 80°C and a beaker of water at 20°C go into an insulated container together. Before any arithmetic, sketch temperature against time for each object.

  • Must the two temperatures meet?
  • Must they meet exactly halfway?
  • Could the lines cross?
  • Why does the transfer of energy stop when it does, rather than earlier or later?

Every one of those questions is answerable from a sketch, well before a single number gets attached to anything.

02

What "equilibrium" actually means

The word deserves an operational meaning rather than a restatement of what it is trying to explain.

Thermal equilibrium: the macrostate in which there is no systematic net transfer of thermal energy between the objects.

That is a subtly stronger claim than saying the two objects simply end up at the same temperature. Equal temperature is how equilibrium gets recognised from the outside; it is not, on its own, an explanation of why the energy stopped moving.

03

The first surprise: not necessarily halfway

Two thought experiments, changing only one detail between them.

case A

identical cups of water, one at 80°C, one at 20°C. Roughly 50°C feels like a safe prediction.

case B

a tiny hot copper ball at 80°C dropped into a large beaker of water at 20°C. Is 50°C still plausible?

It plainly is not. A large reservoir of water is harder to move in temperature than a small copper ball is, which is enough to introduce a new idea informally, without reaching for a formula yet: some objects respond to a given amount of energy with a large change in temperature, others with a small one. That responsiveness deserves its own name, thermal capacity, well before it deserves an equation, and well before it gets pulled apart into the two separate ingredients that are actually doing the work.

04

A model arrives

Heat lost by the hotter object has to equal heat gained by the cooler one, since the container is insulated and energy has nowhere else to go. Before that balance can be written down properly, thermal capacity itself needs separating into the two ingredients that were quietly bundled together in stage three.

How much a given object resists changing temperature depends on how much of it there is, its mass m, and on a property of the material itself, independent of quantity, called specific heat capacity, c: the energy needed to raise one kilogram of that substance by one kelvin. The object's total heat capacity, C, is simply the product of the two.

C = mc      [C] = J·K−1      [c] = J·kg−1·K−1

c is intensive, the same number for a teaspoon of copper as for a girder of it; C is extensive, and grows with however much material is actually present. The heat balance is properly a balance of these total capacities.

m₁c₁(T₁ − Teq) = m₂c₂(Teq − T₂)
Teq = (m₁c₁T₁ + m₂c₂T₂) ⁄ (m₁c₁ + m₂c₂)

Water's resistance to temperature change in stage three was never only a matter of there being more of it. Water also happens to carry an unusually large specific heat capacity as a material, roughly 4200 J·kg−1·K−1 against copper's roughly 385, nearly eleven times as stubborn kilogram for kilogram, so a bigger mass and a more stubborn material were compounding, not a single effect wearing two names.

05

Energy conservation isn't enough

Imagine, purely as a thought experiment, a Tuesday on which the copper ball spontaneously grows hotter while the water cools further, energy quietly flowing the wrong way. Every joule that leaves the water arrives in the copper; the books balance exactly.

Ask directly: what law of physics has been violated? The honest first guess is usually conservation of energy, and it is worth sitting with the fact that this scenario does not actually break it. Something else is doing the forbidding, and it has nothing to do with bookkeeping.

06

Counting returns

Model each object as a handful of tiny energy stores, oscillators, each able to hold any whole number of identical energy quanta. A small copper ball gets a small number of these stores; a larger, more absorbent object gets more of them. The total number of quanta shared between the two objects is fixed, since energy is conserved, but exactly how those quanta split between object A and object B is still completely open.

qA + qB = Q      Ωtotal(qA) = ΩA(qA) × ΩB(qB)

07

The majority verdict

Three questions, asked in order, of the activity above.

  • Did anything push the quanta toward the dominant split? No.
  • Was energy conserved throughout every split that was tried? Yes.
  • So why is that one split special? Because it corresponds to an overwhelming majority of the microscopic ways the energy could have been arranged, while every other split corresponds to comparatively few.

Nothing in the counting exercise resembles a force. The dominant split wins by sheer numerical weight, the same way a fair coin tossed a thousand times overwhelmingly lands near five hundred heads without anything nudging it there.

08

Where temperature comes from

The stages above are enough to explain why equilibrium happens without ever mentioning temperature as a starting assumption. What is left, as an extension rather than a requirement, is to show that equal temperature falls out of the same argument rather than being fed into it.

Reveal where equal temperature comes from

At the dominant split, nudging a small amount of energy dE from A to B changes the total count by nothing at all, since that split was already the maximum.

dΩtotal = 0   ⇒   d(ln ΩA)⁄dEA = d(ln ΩB)⁄dEB

Recall entropy as kB times the logarithm of Ω, and the condition becomes a statement about entropy responding equally to added energy on both sides.

dSA⁄dEA = dSB⁄dEB

Thermodynamics defines temperature through exactly this derivative, one over T as how entropy responds to a small addition of energy.

1⁄T ≡ dS⁄dE   ⇒   TA = TB at equilibrium

Equal temperature is not the definition of equilibrium here; it is a consequence, arrived at through a short chain that starts with plain counting.

microstates, Ω→ entropy, S = kB ln Ω→ temperature, 1⁄T = dS⁄dE
·

The reframing

observed

a single temperature, once the copper and the water settle: the macrostate

inferred

an overwhelming majority of hidden energy splits agreeing on roughly that value: the microstates

Equilibrium, understood this way, has nothing to do with a system running out of things to do; it is the state that almost everything the system could be doing happens to look like from the outside, which takes entropy a long way from disorder and puts it squarely behind an ordinary, stable, boring cup of lukewarm water.
