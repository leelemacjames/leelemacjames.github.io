A staged problem — mechanics & variational reasoning

The Fastest Fall

Two fixed points, a frictionless bead, and a question whose famous answer is, for once, not the point of asking it.

Points A and B are fixed in a vertical plane, with B lower than A. A small bead, released from rest at A, slides without friction along whatever track connects the two points. The question this page stages, in ten stages, is which shape of track carries the bead from A to B in the least possible time.

A model can name the answer to this problem in a matter of seconds, and naming it is close to the least useful thing anyone could do with it here. What follows treats that as an opening rather than an obstacle: the intellectual work was never really in knowing the word for the curve, but in building, piece by piece, the reasons for believing one shape beats another.

01

Qualitative prediction

The straight line is the shortest path between A and B, though shortest is not the same claim as fastest. A track that drops steeply near the start lets the bead pick up speed early, even if the price of that early speed is a longer road to travel afterward, and this tension between distance and speed is what the rest of the page tries to make precise.

Before any calculation, sketch three candidate tracks: a straight line, a curve that dips well below it, and a curve that stays close above it. Predict which one wins, and hold on to the reasons rather than the guess.

02

Energy

At a vertical drop h below the start, conservation of energy gives half the bead's mass times its speed squared equal to its weight times that drop, so the mass cancels and the speed depends only on how far below A the bead currently sits.

½mv² = mgh  →  v = √(2gh)

This single relation already gives away a surprising amount without touching the shape of the track at all: the mass never mattered, the speed is zero exactly at the starting point, and dropping early is rewarded immediately, since speed depends only on depth already fallen, not on how that depth was reached.

03

Time as many small pieces

For a short segment of track of length Δs, travelled at roughly constant speed v, the time taken is just distance over speed, and the total journey time is the sum of all such pieces.

Δt = Δs ⁄ v     T = Σ Δs ⁄ v

None of this needs calculus yet. A candidate track drawn on squared paper can be cut into segments by hand, each segment's speed estimated from the energy relation above, and an approximate travel time built up piece by piece. The continuous version below is only the limit of that same sum as the pieces shrink.

T = ∫AB ds ⁄ v
04

Compare restricted families

Rather than asking for the best curve among all possible curves, it helps to ask smaller, answerable questions within limited families: straight lines through different intermediate points, two-segment tracks with a movable elbow, circular arcs of varying radius, or power-law curves of the form y ∝ xn for different n. Each of these turns an apparently impossible problem into a finite, checkable comparison.

05

Experiment

A practical version needs only flexible tubing or track, a marble or ball bearing, a phone camera for timing, and a few fixed endpoints tried with differently shaped tracks. Real apparatus introduces complications that the idealised problem hides: the ball rolls rather than slides, friction is never quite zero, timing has real uncertainty, the track is never quite the shape it was meant to be, and a fast enough bead can leave the track altogether.

Rolling changes the energy balance directly, since some energy goes into spin rather than translation. For a solid sphere of radius r, with moment of inertia 2⁄5mr² and angular speed ω = v⁄r, conservation of energy gives:

½mv² + ½Iω² = mgh  →  v = √(10gh ⁄ 7)

The speed-depth relation changes by a constant factor compared with frictionless sliding; whether the optimal shape of track changes as well is itself a fair conjecture to test, not something to assume either way.

06

Dimensional analysis

Suppose the whole problem has a vertical scale H and a horizontal scale L. The only quantities available are H, L, and g, and from H and g alone the natural unit of time is √(H⁄g). Whatever the true minimum time turns out to be, it must take the form:

T = √(H⁄g) · f(L⁄H)

for some dimensionless function f of the aspect ratio alone. This does not find the track, but it rules out every proposed answer that does not have this shape, which is a genuine constraint obtained before any optimisation at all.

07

Scaling

If every length in the diagram is stretched by a factor λ, speeds scale as √λ, since speed depends on the square root of a drop, while distances scale as λ itself. Time, being distance over speed, therefore scales as √λ as well.

T(λ) = √λ · T(1)

A track twice as large in every dimension, with λ = 2, does not take twice as long to traverse; it takes √2, or roughly 1.41, times as long. That is a firm, checkable conclusion, reached without solving the optimisation problem at all.

08

Refraction analogy

Divide the vertical region into thin horizontal layers, and within each layer treat the bead's speed as roughly constant. The upper layers, where the bead is still slow, behave like a slow optical medium, while the lower layers behave like a fast one. The fastest route through layered media of this kind is governed by a Snell-like condition, familiar from optics, relating the angle of travel in each layer to the local speed.

sinθ ⁄ v = constant

The natural question this raises is whether the bead's path bends for exactly the reason light bends crossing between media of different propagation speed, which turns a mechanics problem into an optics problem without changing a single equation.

09

Numerical optimisation

A track can be represented as a finite set of movable points between A and B; the total time is estimated from the discrete sum introduced in stage three, one point is nudged at a time, and any change that lowers the total is kept. This can be done by hand, in a spreadsheet, in a short script, with a physical string-and-pin model, or through an evolutionary search. Whatever curve emerges is evidence to interpret and question, not an answer to copy down.

10

Limiting cases

Any working conjecture should survive being pushed to its edges. Worth testing against each of the following, without necessarily answering all of them:

  • What happens as B moves to sit almost directly below A?
  • What happens as B moves very far horizontally from A?
  • Should the track leave A horizontally, vertically, or at some other angle?
  • Can a track usefully dip below the level of B and rise again to reach it?
  • Would a sharp corner, a cusp, in the track ever be physically sensible?
  • Can an arbitrarily steep initial drop make the total time arbitrarily small?

Seven ways to be productively stuck

Being unable to solve the full problem is not the same as having nothing to show for the attempt. Each of the moves below counts as real progress on its own terms.

01

Eliminate a possibility

"The straight line is not always fastest."

02

Identify an invariant

"The mass of the bead does not matter."

03

Identify a scale

"The characteristic time is proportional to √(H⁄g)."

04

Produce evidence

"A two-segment path with a steep first section beats the direct line here."

05

Translate the problem

"This resembles light crossing layers where speed varies with depth."

06

Refine the model

"A real marble rolls, so rotational kinetic energy has to be included."

07

Formulate the missing question

"I need to know how changing a small piece of the path changes the total time."

The classroom formulation

Task sheet

The Fastest Fall

A bead starts from rest at A and must reach the lower point B by sliding along a frictionless track. Design the fastest possible track.

You are not expected to find the exact curve. Your task is to establish as many defensible facts about it as possible.

You may:

  • compare particular tracks;
  • derive relevant equations;
  • construct an experiment;
  • build a numerical model;
  • use dimensional analysis;
  • examine limiting cases;
  • identify analogies with other areas of physics;
  • formulate conjectures;
  • disprove plausible claims.

Every substantial claim must be accompanied by a reason, calculation, measurement, diagram, simulation, or counterexample.

This is, in miniature, the same discipline that keeps an unsolved problem in number theory worth returning to for decades: the point was never to arrive at the named result by the end of the lesson, but to leave with more true things established than the class walked in with.
