Bayesian π

A point estimate tells us where our simulation has landed. A posterior distribution tells us how uncertain that landing still is.

Introduction

Although I usually prefer the day/month/year calendar format, month/day/year offers one distinctive advantage: it gives us 3/14, officially recognised as Pi Day in the United States and also celebrated in Belize.

A common Monte Carlo tutorial estimates \(\pi\) by generating random points in a square and measuring the proportion that fall inside an inscribed circle. Repeating the experiment produces estimates that tend to cluster around the true value.

Such demonstrations often stop at a point estimate. Bayesian inference goes further: it produces a probability distribution over the plausible values of \(\pi\), allowing the uncertainty to contract as observations accumulate.

The simulation uses a fixed seed derived from the digits of \(\pi\): 314159. The browser implementation below uses a deterministic pseudo-random generator, so the results are reproducible.

Sampling \(\pi\)

Sample points \((x,y)\) uniformly from the square \([-1,1]\times[-1,1]\). A point is counted as a success when \[ x^2+y^2<1. \]

The unit circle has area \(\pi\), while the square has area \(4\). Therefore the probability \(q\) that a point lands inside the circle is

\[ q=\frac{\pi}{4} \approx \frac{\text{points inside the circle}}{\text{total points}}. \]

Hence the familiar Monte Carlo estimate is \[ \widehat{\pi}=4\frac{s}{N}, \] where \(s\) is the number of successes among \(N\) sampled points.

Beyond the point estimate

Each point produces a Bernoulli outcome: success if it falls inside the circle and failure otherwise. Given \(q\), the number of successes follows a binomial model:

\[ p(s\mid q,N)=\binom Ns q^s(1-q)^{N-s}. \]

Posterior

Begin with the uniform prior \(q\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)\). Beta–binomial conjugacy then gives the exact posterior

\[ q\mid s,N \sim \operatorname{Beta}(s+1,N-s+1). \]

This avoids numerical underflow and eliminates the need to evaluate a million-point grid. The density is computed in log space in the interactive figure.

Transforming the posterior to \(\pi\)

Since \(\pi=4q\), a change of variables gives

\[ p(\pi\mid s,N) = \frac14\,p_q\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\,\middle|\,s,N\right), \qquad 0\leq \pi\leq4. \]

The right-hand plot shows this transformed posterior. The vertical line marks the true value of \(\pi\), and the shaded area is an equal-tailed 95% Bayesian credible interval.

A 95% credible interval means that, under the stated model and prior, the posterior probability assigned to values of \(\pi\) inside the interval is 0.95. It is not a claim that repeated intervals contain a fixed parameter 95% of the time.

Visualising the update

With only a few samples, the posterior is wide and can be strongly skewed. In the seeded run used here, the earliest points may all land inside the circle, so the point estimate can temporarily equal \(4\). The posterior records that this estimate is still highly uncertain.

As \(N\) grows, the likelihood contributes increasing information. The posterior narrows, the credible interval contracts, and its centre moves towards the true value of \(\pi\). This is the essential Bayesian advantage: uncertainty is represented explicitly rather than hidden behind a single reported number.
