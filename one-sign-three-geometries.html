One Sign, Three Geometries

The elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic trichotomy, from conics to the cube roots of unity

Lee McCulloch-James · McMurmerings · October 2026

One square, three signs

The sign of a single square sorts a surprising amount of mathematics into three families, and the families have names far older than the textbooks that use them: elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic. The cleanest place to see the sorting is a 2 × 2 matrix M with trace τ and determinant δ, whose characteristic polynomial is λ² − τλ + δ and whose discriminant is τ² − 4δ.

The Cayley–Hamilton theorem says M satisfies its own characteristic equation, and shifting M by half its trace removes the linear term, leaving

\[\left(M - \tfrac{\tau}{2}I\right)^2 = \tfrac14\left(\tau^2 - 4\delta\right)I\]

so every such matrix is a multiple of the identity plus a part whose square is negative, zero or positive. Rescaled, that part is a matrix J with J² = −I, J² = 0 or J² = +I, and these three equations are the seeds of the complex numbers, the dual numbers and the split-complex numbers, of rotation, shear and squeeze, and of the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola.

Conics: one formula with a sign in it

The relations b² = a²(1 − e²) for the ellipse and b² = a²(e² − 1) for the hyperbola are a single relation, b² = a²|1 − e²|, read on either side of e = 1. The parabola sits at the boundary, where 1 − e² vanishes and the second focus has run off to infinity; holding one focus and the nearer vertex fixed while e climbs towards 1 stretches an ellipse into a parabola, and pushing e past 1 tears it open into the near branch of a hyperbola.

Each family has a most symmetric member. The circle is the ellipse with e = 0, and the rectangular hyperbola xy = c², with e = √2, is the hyperbola whose asymptotes meet at right angles, so that its two axes play interchangeable roles in the way a circle's diameters do.

The rectangular hyperbola also leads somewhere further. Written in polar form, x² − y² = a² becomes r² cos 2θ = a², and inverting in the circle of radius a, which replaces r by a²/r, gives the lemniscate

\[r^2 = a^2\cos 2\theta\]

The polar area formula gives each loop an area of a²/2, and the polar arc length formula, after the simplification r² + (dr/dθ)² = a⁴/r², gives a quarter of the curve the length

\[s = \int_0^a \frac{a^2\,dr}{\sqrt{a^4 - r^4}} = \frac{a\varpi}{2},\qquad \varpi = 2\int_0^1 \frac{dt}{\sqrt{1 - t^4}} \approx 2.62206\]

which sits beside π = 2∫₀¹ dt/√(1 − t²), the same integral with a fourth power where the circle has a square. Nothing beyond polar area, the conics and arc length is needed to reach ϖ.

Circular and hyperbolic functions

The standard parametric forms work because of two identities: the point (a cos θ, b sin θ) lies on the ellipse since cos²θ + sin²θ = 1, the point (a cosh θ, b sinh θ) lies on the hyperbola since cosh²θ − sinh²θ = 1, and the parabola needs no special function at all, since (at², 2at) satisfies y² = 4ax by plain algebra. The parabolic column is polynomial, which matches its exponential, exp(εt) = 1 + εt, a series that stops after two terms because ε² = 0.

The Maclaurin series show the same split from the inside. The series for sin x and cos x alternate in sign, while those for sinh x and cosh x, obtainable by halving the sum and difference of eˣ and e⁻ˣ, carry every sign positive, so that cos(ix) = cosh x and the identity cosh 2x = cosh²x + sinh²x is the familiar cos 2x = cos²x − sin²x with each sin² replaced by −sinh². The parameter has the same meaning in both columns, since the polar area formula gives the sector of the unit circle out to angle θ an area of θ/2, and the region between the unit hyperbola x² − y² = 1, the x-axis and the ray to (cosh t, sinh t) has area t/2 as well.

The standard table of integrals then reads as a sorting machine. Under the square root, a² − x² sends the integral to arcsin(x/a), while x² + a² and x² − a² send it to arsinh(x/a) and arcosh(x/a), so the sign of the quadratic under the root decides whether the answer is circular or hyperbolic, which is the trichotomy again, this time deciding an integral.

Matrices and number systems

The rotation and reflection matrices fall on opposite sides of the divide. The rotation matrix has trace 2 cos θ and determinant 1, so its discriminant 4cos²θ − 4 is never positive, and it can be written as cos θ I + sin θ J with J the rotation through a right angle, J² = −I. Matrices of the form aI + bJ add and multiply exactly as the complex numbers a + bi do, and de Moivre's theorem is the statement that powers of a rotation are rotations through multiplied angles.

The reflection matrix has trace 0 and determinant −1, so its discriminant is 4 and it squares to the identity, which places it in the hyperbolic column with eigenvalues ±1 along the mirror line and its perpendicular. Its close relative is the squeeze, cosh t I + sinh t K with K² = I, the transformation that preserves x² − y² and so slides points along a rectangular hyperbola, and that physics students later meet as the Lorentz boost; the split-complex analogue of de Moivre, (cosh t + j sinh t)ⁿ = cosh nt + j sinh nt, holds by the same argument. Between them sits the shear, I + tN with N² = 0, whose powers simply add their parameters and whose single eigenvalue is repeated.

The same structure underlies the quadratic rings. Multiplication by x + y√d on the basis {1, √d} has the matrix

\[\begin{pmatrix} x & dy \\ y & x \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det = x^2 - dy^2\]

whose determinant is the norm, positive definite when d < 0, as for the Gaussian integers, and indefinite when d > 0, as for ℤ[√2], where the units 1 + √2 and its powers march out along a hyperbola x² − 2y² = ±1. Anyone who can multiply 2 × 2 matrices and compute a determinant already holds the tools to see why a real quadratic ring has infinitely many units while an imaginary one has finitely many: one norm curve is an ellipse with finitely many lattice points on it, and the other is a hyperbola with infinitely many.

Roots of unity and a weakness for threes

The roots of zⁿ = 1 are exp(2πki/n), and the case n = 3 gives the elliptic column its most symmetric number system. The primitive cube root of unity ω = exp(2πi/3) satisfies ω² + ω + 1 = 0, a quadratic with discriminant −3, and the Eisenstein integers a + bω fill the plane as a triangular lattice with norm a² − ab + b², the hexagonal partner of the square lattice of the Gaussian integers, whose defining quadratic x² + 1 has discriminant −4.

These two lattices are to the elliptic column what the circle and the rectangular hyperbola are to the conics, its points of greatest symmetry, and they reappear in the curves. The lemniscate's constant ϖ belongs to an elliptic curve whose symmetries include multiplication by i, so it is tied to the Gaussian integers, while the corresponding curve with a threefold symmetry is tied to ℤ[ω] in exactly the same way.

The threefold structure survives reduction too, and in a form a student can check by hand. Dividing ℤ[ω] by 1 − ω, which has norm 3, leaves the field with three elements, while dividing by 2 leaves a field with four, {0, 1, ω, ω²}, in which ω² = ω + 1 because signs no longer matter modulo 2. Read that way, the defining relation of ω has become x² = x + 1, the equation of the golden ratio, so that within this four-element field the cube root of unity and the golden ratio obey the same law.

Cardano's formula for the cubic needs ω as well: when a cubic has three real roots, the formula can only reach them by passing through complex cube roots and their rotations by ω and ω², so even a wholly real problem is routed through the elliptic column.

Coda: one question to carry

Conics, hyperbolic functions, matrices and complex numbers are usually taught as separate topics and, for most students, stored in separate compartments of memory. Each of those topics, read with the question of which sign a governing square takes, turns out to state one case of a single fact, and a formula recalled as a case of that fact can be reconstructed when memory fails, since the missing sign or function follows from the column it belongs to.

The habit costs little to teach. Faced with a new formula, a student asks what is being squared, whether that square is added, cancelled or subtracted, and what the companion in each of the other two columns looks like; the standard tables usually record two of the three, and the third, more often than not, is the one worth deriving. The parabolic column, rarely printed because its formulas are polynomial, is where that derivation is easiest, and the elliptic column at n = 3, where the circle, the cube roots of unity and the hexagonal lattice coincide, is where it opens furthest onto deeper mathematics.
