Which Way Round

A transformation matrix sends the unit square to a parallelogram, and its determinant records two things at once: how much area the image has, and whether the corners still run anticlockwise. Drag the images of the two basis vectors below and watch the sign of that area keep score of handedness, then turn to the gallery of standard transformations, redrawn so that the orientation is visible.

The lab

The columns of the matrix are where (1, 0) and (0, 1) land. The curved arrow at the origin turns from the first image to the second; while it turns anticlockwise the determinant is positive, and the moment the second image swings past the line of the first the arrow reverses and the area changes sign.

The gallery

The standard transformations, each with its parameters live. Two entries differ from the usual poster: the rotation is written for column vectors, so it turns anticlockwise as its angle increases, and the map that sends (x, y) to (−x, −y) is shown as what its determinant says it is, a half-turn.

Composition

Applying A and then B gives the product BA, and its oriented area is the product of the two: area factors multiply, and so do orientations, which is why two reflections always make a rotation. The selections below use each card's current settings.
