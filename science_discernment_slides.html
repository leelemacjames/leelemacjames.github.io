The Furniture of Science
Forces. Fields. Atoms. Stars.
Waves. Cells. Climate. Energy.

These are the furniture of a science course, and they matter. But learning the names of the furniture is not the same as learning how to look at the room.

Ontology

What kind of thing is this?

Before we calculate, we quietly make decisions about what exists in our model.

  • Object
  • System
  • Property
  • Interaction
  • Process
  • Field
  • Boundary
  • Measurement
  • Model

Science begins by learning not to muddle these categories.

The Object Is Not Its Description
A ball has mass.
It moves with a velocity.
It may interact through a gravitational field.
It may transfer energy.

A graph of its motion is not the motion. An equation describing it is not the object. A model is something we use to reason about the world.

Good science keeps the world, the measurement, and the model distinct.

Science Makes Belief Expensive
A claim is easy.
A plausible explanation is easy.
A striking example is easy.

What evidence would entitle us to believe this?
And then: how strongly?

Good Science? · 01

A student measures the period of a pendulum once: T = 1.42 s. They report:

“The period of this pendulum is exactly 1.42 s.”

Decide, then click to reveal · Discernment

No

The measurement may be 1.42 s. The period itself has not thereby become exactly 1.42 s. Ask: how repeatable is the measurement, what is the timer resolution, what variation appears across trials, does 1.42 s represent more precision than the experiment supports?

A measured number is evidence about a quantity, not the quantity itself.

Good Science? · 02

A class investigates resistance between 20°C and 60°C. The graph is approximately linear. A student concludes:

“At 3000°C the resistance will therefore be given by the same straight line.”

Decide, then click to reveal · Extrapolation

Not without further justification

The student has extrapolated far beyond the measured domain. A model that behaves well from 20–60°C has earned some trust there. It has not automatically earned trust at 3000°C, where material properties may change, the object may melt, or the assumed relationship may cease to apply.

Models have domains.

Good Science? · 03

Measurements give 20°C → 11.2 Ω, 30°C → 12.0 Ω, 40°C → 12.8 Ω. A student estimates the resistance at 35°C as about 12.4 Ω.

Decide, then click to reveal · Interpolation

Reasonable, with qualification

35°C lies inside the measured range. This is interpolation, generally less hazardous than extrapolation because nearby observations constrain the estimate. But it still assumes the behaviour between measurements is sufficiently smooth.

Interpolation is an inference too.

Good Science? · 04

Twenty trials are performed. Eighteen measurements lie between 9.7 and 10.3. Two are 8.1 and 11.8. The student removes the two unusual values because:

“They spoil the graph.”

Decide, then click to reveal · Anomalies

No

An unusual result may arise from measurement error, an uncontrolled variable, equipment failure, or a genuine feature of the phenomenon. There may be a defensible reason to exclude a datum. “It makes my conclusion weaker” is not one.

A good scientist asks why it differs before deciding what to do with it.

Good Science? · 05

A student believes plants grow faster with classical music. They search online and collect six experiments in which plants exposed to music grew more, and present these six as evidence that the hypothesis is correct.

Decide, then click to reveal · Cherry-picking

No

The question is not “can I find evidence consistent with my claim”, for almost any popular claim, perhaps you can. The question is what the relevant body of evidence shows. Searching selectively for confirming cases is cherry-picking.

Science asks us to seek the evidence that could make our preferred claim fail.

Anecdote ≠ Representative Evidence

“My grandfather smoked and lived to 96.”

This may be completely true. It is also weak evidence about the effect of smoking on mortality, because the scientific question concerns a population and a difference in risk. A memorable individual is not necessarily a representative sample.

Anecdotes can generate questions.
Representative evidence tests them.

Good Science? · 06

A video shows one electric car catching fire. Someone concludes:

“Electric cars are more likely to catch fire than petrol cars.”

What's missing? · Click to reveal · The denominator

Incomplete

We need comparison: how many fires occurred out of how many electric cars, and out of how many comparable petrol cars? Over what distance or time, under what conditions, with what reporting method? A numerator can frighten us. A rate allows comparison.

Good science asks: compared with what?

Good Science? · 07

Students who sleep longer obtain higher examination scores. A headline says:

“Extra sleep causes higher grades.”

Decide, then click to reveal · Association vs mechanism

Not yet established

The observation may be sound; the causal claim requires more. Perhaps sleep improves performance. Perhaps students with lower stress both sleep more and perform better. Perhaps age, workload, health, school, or socioeconomic factors contribute. The correlation is not useless, it is a clue.

Science distinguishes finding a relationship from explaining it.

Good Science? · 08

A magnet attracts an iron nail. A student writes:

“The nail moves because the magnet attracts it.”

Explanation or description? · Click to reveal

Redescription

We have given the observed behaviour a name: attraction. A deeper physical account might introduce a magnetic field, the response of the material to that field, a field gradient, and the resulting net force.

Repeating the observation with more technical vocabulary does not automatically explain it.

Good Science? · 09

Two graphs have the same mathematical shape. A student says:

“They must be the same physical phenomenon.”

Decide, then click to reveal · Structure vs ontology

No — but note the analogy

Different systems can instantiate the same mathematical relationship, one of science's great powers. But same equation does not mean same object. A spring and an electrical circuit may both oscillate; heat and particles may both diffuse. The structural analogy can illuminate. The ontological distinction still matters.

Unify what can be unified. Distinguish what must be distinguished.

Good Science? · 10

A result agrees with the student's prediction. They write:

“This proves my hypothesis.”

Decide, then click to reveal · Warrant vs proof

Usually, no
  • Is the result consistent with the hypothesis?
  • How precise was the test, and was there a control?
  • What competing hypotheses predict the same result?
  • Has the result been replicated?

Science usually deals in degrees of warrant, not ceremonial declarations of proof.

The Discernment Check

When somebody makes a scientific claim, ask:

What exactly was observed?
What has been inferred from it?
What kind of thing is being discussed?
Compared with what?
How was the sample selected?
Is the claim inside or outside the measured range?
Could another explanation produce the same observation?
What evidence would count against the claim?
How much confidence does the evidence actually warrant?
The Sentence That May Save You
“This proves that…”
“This provides evidence that…”

Then force yourself to finish the sentence carefully. How much evidence? Under what conditions? Against which alternatives? Within what limits?

What This Course Is Really Practising

Yes, you will learn equations, facts, and how to solve problems. But beneath all of this we are practising something more durable, to:

notice carefully;
categorise intelligently;
compare fairly;
infer cautiously;
seek structure across difference;
demand evidence proportional to a claim;
and change our minds when the world requires it.

That is the virtue of discernment.
And that is part of what it means to learn science.
