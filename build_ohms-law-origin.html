Where Does Ohm's Law Come From?

A law usually handed over as voltage equals current times resistance, rebuilt instead from a single charge carrier bouncing through a lattice.

Rather than beginning from the finished statement of Ohm's law, it is worth starting much smaller: imagine a single charge carrier inside a conductor, and ask what actually makes it drift, what slows it down, and why it does not simply accelerate forever under a steady field.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no V = IR handed over as a starting point
  • no assumption that Ohm's law is a law of nature in the way Coulomb's is
01

What makes it drift?

A charge carrier sitting inside a conductor with an electric field switched on feels a steady force, and a steady force on a free carrier should mean a steadily increasing speed, exactly as it would for any charged particle in empty space.

a = qE ⁄ m

Left alone, that acceleration would let the carrier's speed grow without bound, which is plainly not what happens in an ordinary wire carrying an ordinary current.

02

What slows it down?

A real conductor is not empty space; it is a lattice of atoms, impurities, and vibrations, and a moving carrier collides with that structure over and over, each collision scrambling its direction of travel. Between one collision and the next, the field has only a short average time, called the collision time, to do any accelerating at all.

This is why the carrier does not accelerate forever: it is constantly being reset. What survives, once the resetting is averaged over many collisions, is a small steady drift superimposed on a much larger, randomly directed motion.

04

From drift to current

Averaged over the collision time, the field manages to build up a small mean velocity before each reset, called the drift velocity.

vd = aτ = qEτ ⁄ m

It is convenient to collect the carrier's own properties into a single number called its mobility, so that drift velocity becomes simply mobility times field.

μ = qτ ⁄ m      vd = μE

Current density is charge per carrier, times how many carriers there are per unit volume, times how fast they drift, which lands on a relationship between current density and field that looks exactly like Ohm's law, because it is one.

J = nqvd = nqμE = σE
05

Ohm's law as a constitutive law

Every quantity in that last line except the field itself, the carrier density, the collision time, the mobility, describes a property of the particular material, not a universal constant of nature. That makes Ohm's law a fundamentally different kind of statement from Coulomb's law.

interaction law

holds between any two charges, anywhere, built from geometry and a universal constant

constitutive law

describes how a particular material happens to respond, valid only in the regime it was measured in

Ohm's law belongs in the second category, alongside Hooke's law for a spring: both are linear-response approximations that hold well over some range of conditions and quietly stop holding outside it, rather than exact statements about the structure of the universe. Treating the two kinds of law as interchangeable, just because both are eventually written down as a tidy equation, is exactly the habit worth unlearning here.
