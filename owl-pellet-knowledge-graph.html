Notes for the lesson

The move being taught

A food web is a knowledge graph in which every edge happens to carry the same label, which is why removing a species from a food web always looks like the same kind of event. Once edges are typed, removal stops being uniform; taking out a pollinator propagates along a different set of paths from taking out a predator, and the class can watch that happen with the relation switches rather than being told it.

Sequence that works

  • Dissect, identify, and enter only the evidence layer: contains, identifies, found_in. Nobody has said anything about eating yet.
  • Ask what the graph currently claims. It claims a barn owl ate a vole only if somebody adds that edge, which surfaces the difference between a bone in a pellet and an interpretation of it.
  • Add the ecological layer: eats, competes_with, shelters_in. The graph now mixes observation with inference, and the colours keep the two visible.
  • Knock out a node. Ask who is orphaned, and whether the orphaning is a fact about the ecosystem or a fact about the dataset.

The geography crossing

Degree and betweenness are the same two numbers you would compute for a bus network or a street grid, and the high scorers behave the same way in all three settings. A market town with two roads can have enormous betweenness, exactly as a single enzyme can be a metabolic chokepoint without being a busy one. Both quantities are countable by hand for a graph this size, so the unit gets its power-law intuitions without needing logarithms.

Honest caveats to plant

  • Pellet contents are biased towards prey with durable bones, so absence in the graph is weak evidence of absence in the diet.
  • Co-occurrence inside a pellet is a statement about one owl's evening, not about the two prey species having any relationship at all; the same confusion runs through biomedical knowledge graphs built from co-mention in papers.
  • A misidentified jaw creates a real edge to a fictional species, and the graph will happily compute metrics on it.
