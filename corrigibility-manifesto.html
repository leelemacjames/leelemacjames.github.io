A Corrigible Position

Terms of argument for a staffroom deciding what artificial intelligence is for, written on the assumption that the decision will turn out to be partly wrong and that we would like to find out which part.

Preamble

Discussions about AI in schools tend to arrive already sorted into camps, and the sorting happens so early that the arguments which follow are decorative; the enthusiast has heard the objection before it is made, the sceptic has heard the answer, and the meeting produces a decision that nobody's mind was changed by. What follows is an attempt to make the disagreement do some work, which requires first that people disagree out loud, and second that they disagree in a form which could turn out to be mistaken.

This is a document about how to argue, not about what to conclude. It takes no view on whether the technology belongs in a classroom.

The word, and its more familiar cousin
incorrigible adjective · ordinary English

Beyond correction or reform. Said of an incurable habit, or of a person who cannot be set straight; an incorrigible liar, an incorrigible optimist. The tone is usually half-exasperated and half-fond, though older legal usage was colder and meant an offender considered past saving. From the Latin corrigere, to set straight, with the negative prefix.

corrigible adjective · rare, and lately revived

The affirmative form, which English very nearly lost; capable of being corrected. It was dusted off by researchers working on machine safety, where a corrigible system is one that does not resist being stopped, inspected or changed, on the reasoning that a system able to protect itself from correction has made its own errors permanent.

Borrowed here for a room of people rather than a machine, it means something quite specific and quite demanding: a corrigible position is one held together with the conditions under which its holder would give it up. The holder can say what evidence would count against them, and can say it in advance, before knowing whether the evidence will appear.

The distinction that matters is therefore not between agreement and disagreement, both of which can be entirely useless, but between positions that remain answerable and positions that have quietly stopped being so. A dissenter who could never be satisfied contributes no more to a group's thinking than an enthusiast who was always going to approve, and both are less useful than one person who says plainly what would change their mind.

Articles

  1. State the position, and state what would overturn it.

    Every contribution of substance should carry its own defeater; if you believe the marking assistant saves time, name the workload measure you would accept as a test, and if you believe it corrodes judgement, name the thing you would look for in the marking itself. Positions offered without defeaters are declarations of identity, and identity is not something a meeting can adjudicate.

    Guards against · the incorrigible objector, the settled enthusiast

  2. Objections are owed to the room.

    An objection raised in the corridor afterwards has all of the cost of dissent and none of its value; it cannot be answered, it cannot be tested, and it recruits without informing. If the thing is worth saying to a colleague on the walk to the car park, it was worth saying twenty minutes earlier when it could still have altered something.

    Guards against · the quiet whinger

  3. Silence will be recorded as assent.

    The passenger who sits through the discussion uncommitted keeps the option of having been against it all along, and takes the benefits of a decision while bearing none of its authorship; this is a comfortable position and an expensive one for everyone else. Anyone unwilling to be counted as having agreed should say so while the counting is still open.

    Guards against · the passive passenger

  4. Bring the particular case.

    Nobody in the room speaks for students in general, and the arguments that turn on an imagined average pupil are unfalsifiable by construction; bring the year group, the task, the piece of work in front of you, and let the general claim be assembled afterwards from cases that somebody actually saw.

    Guards against · abstraction as a shield

  5. Say whether you are making an empirical claim or a value claim.

    Whether a tool improves recall is a question with an answer; whether we want pupils to hold knowledge in their own heads is a commitment that no amount of data settles. Most bad-tempered exchanges on this subject are two people arguing across that line without noticing it, and the cure is simply to say which one you are doing.

    Guards against · false empiricism, false principle

  6. Pilot before policy, and concretely.

    A trial that runs in one department for a term, with someone named as responsible for reporting what happened including the parts that went badly, is worth more than a position paper; it also has the virtue of being reversible, which is the institutional form of the property this document is named after.

    Guards against · irreversible commitment

  7. Name the loss you are accepting.

    Every version of this decision costs something, whether it is the fluency that comes from struggling unaided or the time that could have gone elsewhere, and the honest advocate names their own losses rather than waiting for an opponent to find them. A proposal with no acknowledged downside has not been examined by the person proposing it.

    Guards against · the undefended enthusiasm

  8. Refusal is a position and owes reasons.

    Declining to use a technology is a legitimate choice with real arguments behind it, and it is subject to exactly the same requirements as adoption; the sceptic must also say what would change their mind, must also name what is being given up, and does not get to treat caution as a default that others carry the burden of dislodging.

    Guards against · caution mistaken for neutrality

  9. Date the decision and set its review in advance.

    Fixing the review point before the outcome is known is what stops the review from being scheduled by whoever is currently winning; a decision made in September and revisited in February, with the criteria written down in September, is a decision that can be corrected without anybody having to lose.

    Guards against · consensus hardening into policy

  10. Record the dissent alongside the decision.

    Minority views written into the record are retrievable later, when the thing that was foreseen either happens or fails to happen, and this is the only mechanism by which a group learns whose scepticism was worth listening to. It also gives the dissenter something better to do than be right in private.

    Guards against · the corridor, again
A register of postures

Four of these are failures and one is the target. They are described plainly because most of us occupy at least one of them by temperament, and the point of naming them is recognition rather than accusation.

The passive passenger

Tell: attends everything, commits to nothing

Present at every meeting and absent from every position, the passenger allows the decision to be made around them and preserves, at no cost, the standing to say later that this was never what they wanted. Their silence is not neutrality; it is a small transfer of risk onto the colleagues who spoke.

The quiet whinger

Tell: the objection surfaces only where it cannot be answered

Often the objection itself is a good one, which is what makes the posture so corrosive; the reservation is deposited in corridors, in messages, in the shared exhalation after the door closes, where it accumulates support without ever being tested. The group ends up governed by an argument it was never permitted to examine.

The incorrigible objector

Tell: the objection precedes the case

Opposition offered as a standing disposition rather than a response, which means it arrives before the proposal is understood and relocates whenever it is answered. Because nothing could satisfy it, it carries no information, and its worst effect is to inoculate the room against dissent in general, so that the one genuine objection when it finally comes is filed under the same heading and dismissed.

The agreeable agreer

Tell: warmth uncorrelated with truth

The obliging colleague whose assent is unconditional and therefore uninformative, since agreement that was always going to be given tells you nothing about the merits of what you proposed. This is the failure mode of pleasant institutions, and of AI systems, which is a coincidence worth sitting with when the subject is what to delegate to them.

The corrigible dissenter

Tell: can say in advance what would change their mind

Persistent enough to be taken seriously and genuine enough that the persistence reads as conviction rather than performance, this is the posture that Charlan Nemeth's work on minority influence found actually improves a group's thinking, generating more options and better decisions, while assigned devil's advocacy largely does not and can entrench the majority by letting it feel it has already heard the case.

The target is narrow. Drift one way and you are a pushover, drift the other and you are noise; holding the position requires conceding the parts that are genuinely conceded, which is the manoeuvre most people find hardest and which is the whole of the difference.

What would make us withdraw this document

A manifesto that demanded defeaters of everyone else while offering none of its own would be self-refuting, so here are ours, written before we know how the year goes.

  • If recorded dissent turns out to be used against the dissenters, in appraisal or in reputation, then Article X is doing harm and goes.
  • If the requirement to state defeaters silences the people least confident in the room while licensing the most confident, the requirement is working backwards and should be dropped.
  • If pre-set review dates become a device for postponing decisions indefinitely rather than correcting them, Article IX has been captured.
  • If, after a year, the discussions run under these terms produce the same decisions as the discussions that preceded them, the document is ceremonial and should be abandoned rather than tidied.

The argument about artificial intelligence in schools will not be settled by this generation of teachers, and the most likely outcome is that we are each right about something and wrong about something adjacent to it. The only version of that outcome worth having is the one where we find out which is which, in time to do something about it, from colleagues who told us to our faces.
