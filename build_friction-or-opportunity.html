A staged problem — circuits & the grammar of quantities

Friction, or Opportunity?

Resistance and conductance describe exactly the same wire, yet hand the imagination two different objects to hold onto.

Ohm's law is usually written with resistance, voltage equal to current times resistance, and school physics rarely mentions that this is a choice rather than a necessity. Many physical systems combine more naturally by adding capacities together than by subtracting obstacles apart, and it is worth asking, before reaching for either word, which one actually measures how easily current flows.

Deliberately withheld, for now
  • no claim that either quantity is the more real one
  • no formula for combining resistors, handed over in advance
01

Two words, one wire

Resistance, R, is the familiar one: it appears directly in Ohm's law, and it grows when a wire opposes current more. Its reciprocal is given its own name, conductance, and its own symbol.

G = 1 ⁄ R

Rewriting Ohm's law in terms of G changes nothing about the physics of the wire, only which quantity is doing the describing.

V = IR      I = GV
02

Resistance sounds like friction

Resistance behaves, in the imagination, like something in the way: a narrow doorway, a rough surface, a queue. A large resistance is a large obstacle, and two obstacles placed one after another are simply added, which already feels correct for two narrow doorways in a row.

03

Conductance sounds like opportunity

Conductance behaves like something closer to capacity: a wide doorway, an open lane, a route. A large conductance is a large opportunity for current, and two open lanes running side by side plainly offer more total capacity than either alone.

resistance

an obstacle to overcome; obstacles in series pile up naturally

conductance

a capacity to use; capacities running in parallel add up naturally

04

Two paths side by side

Set three resistors side by side, as three parallel routes for the same current, and adjust each one below. Watch what happens to the two totals underneath: one is built from a nested fraction that looks faintly mysterious, the other from a plain sum that does not.

05

The formula, unmasked

The two totals above always agree, because they are the same arithmetic, only performed in a different order. The nested fraction on the left is what "capacities to conduct add" looks like once it has been rewritten back in terms of resistance; the plain sum on the right is what it looks like left alone.

1⁄Rtotal = 1⁄R₁ + 1⁄R₂ + 1⁄R₃      ≡      Gtotal = G₁ + G₂ + G₃

The formula on the left is the one most students meet first, and the one most students find mysterious; the formula on the right is the same statement, translated into the language it was always more comfortable speaking.

06

The other direction

Take the very same three resistors from the tool above and imagine them end to end instead of side by side, one path for the current rather than three. The duality flips completely: it is now resistance that adds simply, and conductance that hides behind a nested fraction.

Resistance is the natural language of series circuits; conductance is the natural language of parallel ones. Neither quantity is more fundamental than the other; each is simply the one that speaks plainly about a particular kind of arrangement, and reaches for a nested fraction the moment it is asked to describe the other.
