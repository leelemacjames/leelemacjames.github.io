A hub — organised by what a law depends on, not by which chapter it lives in

What Is It a Function Of?

Position tells you where. Velocity tells you how you're moving. Everything else is what you're made of.

The chapters of a physics course, mechanics, electricity, magnetism, thermal, are organised around apparatus and phenomena, not around mathematical kinship. Gravity and the Coulomb force live in different chapters despite sharing an identical structure; the magnetic force and ordinary drag live in different chapters despite both depending on velocity in ways position-dependent forces never do. Sorted instead by what each law is actually a function of, three families appear, and the sort turns out to cut cleanly across every one of the traditional chapter boundaries.

01

The transferable question

Before any equation gets written down, one question does most of the sorting on its own, and it is the same question regardless of which chapter the phenomenon happens to sit in.

Where are you?position.

How are you moving?velocity.

What are you made of?constitutive response.

Asking which of these a phenomenon answers, before reaching for an equation, is the same diagnostic move that already sits behind identifying entities and relations before quantities: it fixes the functional form a law must take before a single symbol has been written.

05

A second axis

Two earlier investigations resist this sort entirely, because they are not answering "what does this depend on" at all; they are answering a different question, "what quantity is being made as small as possible." That is a genuinely separate organising principle, extremal rather than functional, and it deserves its own axis rather than a forced seat in one of the three families above.

06

Where the two axes meet

The functional axis and an interaction-versus-constitutive split, already drawn out separately, turn out to be the same picture seen from two angles: position-dependent and velocity-dependent laws are both interaction laws, universal between any two objects regardless of material, while state-dependent laws are exactly the constitutive ones.

The Coriolis force is worth a second look before it gets filed away as simply "velocity-dependent" alongside the Lorentz force, because it is not really an interaction at all. It appears only because of a choice of reference frame, an observer standing on a rotating platform rather than watching from outside it, and vanishes completely for the outside observer, with no charge, no mass, no source object responsible for it anywhere. It belongs in the velocity-dependent family by its mathematics, and nowhere near an interaction law by its physics, which is the same observer-dependence question the Lorentz investigation ended on, pushed one step further: some forces are not just seen differently by different observers, they exist for some observers and not for others at all.

The gaps left open above, gravity beside Coulomb, drag and Coriolis beside the Lorentz force, Hooke's law and the ideal gas law beside Ohm's, are exactly the shape a functional taxonomy predicts they should be: each one a law already familiar from its own chapter, waiting to be asked the same question the others were asked here.
