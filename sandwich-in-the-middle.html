The Sandwich in the MiddleEnergy, exergy and the size of the economy

A power station, a supermarket and a hospital all run on the same trick: they take energy in a concentrated form, use it to do work, and hand it back to the world spread thin and warm. What follows traces that trick from a single steam turbine to the whole world economy, using fractions, percentages, standard form and a scatter graph.

Energy is never used up, so what is?

The first law of thermodynamics says that energy is conserved, which raises an awkward question about why anyone pays for it. A litre of petrol and the warm exhaust it becomes contain the same number of joules, yet only the petrol will drive a car up a hill. What you really buy is the ability of energy to do work, and physicists call that ability exergy. Exergy depends on how different something is from its surroundings: steam at 550 °C in a room at 20 °C is full of it, while the same energy smeared through the room as a faint warmth has almost none, because nothing is left to flow downhill.

For heat, the largest share that can ever become work depends only on two temperatures, both measured in kelvin (add 273 to a Celsius temperature):

maximum fraction = 1 − Tenv / Thot

Power-station steam at 550 °C is 823 K, and surroundings at 20 °C are 293 K, so the largest possible fraction is 1 − 293/823 ≈ 0.64. Real stations manage about 0.38, because friction, leaks and heat flowing across large temperature gaps destroy some of the exergy that was available, and each of those losses creates entropy that can never be reversed.

Squeezed between a source and a sink

Life on Earth runs on sunlight, which arrives at about 170 000 TW and leaves again as infrared radiation once plants and animals have taken their share. Fossil fuels are sunlight that plants captured over millions of years and that geology then concentrated into coal, oil and gas, and burning them has let human society run far above the energy each creature could gather for itself. The picture at the top shows the result: a society lifted above its surroundings, fed from a shrinking store of concentrated exergy on one side and dumping its waste into the environment on the other.

The waste comes in two kinds, and the heat is smaller than you might guess. Humanity uses energy at about 1.9 × 1013 W, and spreading that over the Earth's surface area of 5.1 × 1014 m² gives

1.9 × 1013 ÷ 5.1 × 1014 ≈ 3.7 × 10−2 W/m²

The extra carbon dioxide in the atmosphere traps about 2.2 W/m², roughly sixty times as much, so the gas in the exhaust warms the planet far more than the heat itself does. Heat still matters over long periods because energy use keeps growing. At a steady growth of g% a year, a quantity doubles about every 70/g years, which is known as the rule of 70. Growing from 0.037 to 1 W/m² means multiplying by about 27, which lies between four doublings (×16) and five (×32).

Only energy released from a store adds this extra heat. Solar panels and wind turbines divert energy the Earth was already receiving and would have radiated anyway, so they add almost none, whereas fossil fuels, nuclear fission and any future fusion reactor release new heat with every joule they supply.

Tracking a ratio with percentages

The energy a country needs for each pound of output is its energy intensity, found by dividing energy use by GDP. When both parts of a ratio are changing, a handy rule lets you work with growth rates alone: the percentage change of a ratio is approximately the percentage change of the top minus the percentage change of the bottom. If energy use grows by 2% while GDP grows by 3%, energy intensity falls by about 1%. The exact answer comes from multiplying the growth factors,

1.02 ÷ 1.03 ≈ 0.9903, a fall of 0.97%

and the rule works because, for small growth rates, the product of two small percentages is tiny enough to ignore.

How tightly are energy and GDP linked?

Plotting world GDP against world energy use for each year from 1971 to 2017 gives a correlation coefficient of 0.997, which looks almost perfect. The coefficient r runs from −1 to 1 and measures how nearly the points lie on a straight line. The difficulty is that any two quantities that both grow steadily over time will correlate strongly, whether or not they are connected. A fairer test compares the yearly changes, since removing the shared upward trend leaves only the ups and downs, and unrelated wobbles rarely line up by accident. For the real world data the yearly changes still give r = 0.83, and the two series crash together in the oil shocks of the 1970s and the financial crisis of 2008, which is far better evidence of a genuine link than the 0.997. Correlation still carries no direction, though: energy lets factories run, which raises output, and a recession closes factories, which cuts energy use, and both effects push r upward.

Since about 1990, world GDP has grown roughly one percentage point a year faster than world energy use, so by the percentage rule world energy intensity has fallen by about 1% a year. Part of that fall comes from machines turning a larger share of exergy into useful work, and part comes from the way energy is counted.

Counting joules

Energy statistics count fossil fuel by the heat it contains. A power station burning 100 units of coal delivers about 38 units of electricity, yet all 100 units appear in the figures. Wind and solar are counted by the electricity they produce, so 38 units of wind electricity count as 38. Replacing coal with wind therefore lowers measured energy use even when every home receives exactly the same electricity, and the economy appears to need less energy as its energy mix changes.

Questions

A hot spring at 70 °C sits in surroundings at 10 °C. What is the largest fraction of its heat that could become work?

70 °C is 343 K and 10 °C is 283 K, so the fraction is 1 − 283/343 ≈ 0.175, which means at most 17.5 J of work from every 100 J of heat. Low-temperature sources hold little exergy even when they hold a great deal of energy.

Energy use grows by 2% a year for 105 years. Roughly how many times larger does it become?

By the rule of 70 it doubles every 35 years, so 105 years is three doublings, a factor of 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The exact value, 1.02105, is about 8.0.

GDP grows by 4% and energy use by 1.5% in the same year. By how much does energy intensity change?

The rule gives 1.5 − 4 = −2.5%. The exact value is 1.015 ÷ 1.04 − 1 ≈ −2.4%, so intensity falls by about 2.4%.

Why is a correlation of 0.83 between yearly changes more convincing than a correlation of 0.997 between the values themselves?

Any two steadily growing series give a correlation close to 1, as the unrelated series show. Yearly changes remove the shared trend, so a high correlation between them means the two quantities rise and fall together from year to year.

Half of a country's coal electricity is replaced by wind. By what percentage does measured energy use fall, if the country receives the same electricity?

For 100 units of electricity, all coal counts as 100 ÷ 0.38 ≈ 263 units. Half and half counts as 50 ÷ 0.38 + 50 ≈ 182 units. The fall is 81 ÷ 263 ≈ 31%, although nobody uses any less electricity.

The economy's dependence on useful work is as physical as a machine's need for fuel. What remains open is whether that work can come from sources that leave the sink undamaged, and whether it can keep growing for long before its waste heat catches up.
