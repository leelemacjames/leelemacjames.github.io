Fermi 01 · Stacking

How many iPhones, stacked flat on top of one another, would reach the Moon?

Ask yourself
  • What is the relevant dimension of a phone, and is it the one you first thought of?
  • Which of the two numbers do you know better?
  • Does the answer depend on anything except a ratio of two lengths?
Anchors
phone thickness ≈ 10−2 mEarth to Moon ≈ 108 m
One route
N = distance ÷ thickness = 108 ÷ 10−2
N ≈ 1 × 1010 phones

Notice that nothing about phones entered except one length. Most Fermi answers are a ratio wearing a costume.

Fermi 02 · Travel Time

How long would it take to walk to New York from here, assuming you could?

Ask yourself
  • How could you get the distance without looking it up? What does a five-hour time difference tell you?
  • What is a walking pace in metres per second, and how would you check it?
  • Are you walking continuously, or is there a sleeping term? Which assumption did you make silently?
Anchors
Earth circumference ≈ 4 × 107 m5/24 of the way roundwalking ≈ 1.5 m s−11 month ≈ 2.6 × 106 s
One route
d ≈ (5/24) × 4 × 107 ≈ 8 × 106 m
t = d ÷ v ≈ 8 × 106 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 5 × 106 s
t ≈ 2 months of continuous walking

Walking eight hours a day rather than twenty-four triples it. The estimate is only as good as the assumption you did not notice making.

Fermi 03 · Aggregate Mass

What is the total mass of the human population of the Earth?

Ask yourself
  • Is the mean mass of a human the same as the mean mass of an adult?
  • Roughly what fraction of the world is under fifteen?
  • Would this answer float or sink a large ship? Ask what you can compare it against.
Anchors
population ≈ 8 × 109mean mass ≈ 50 kglarge tanker ≈ 3 × 108 kg
One route
M = N × m̄ = 8 × 109 × 50
M ≈ 4 × 1011 kg

Around a thousand of the largest ships ever built. Humanity is, by mass, a rounding error on the biosphere.

Fermi 04 · Counting the Invisible

How many molecules of water are there in a glass holding half a litre?

Ask yourself
  • Which bridge takes you from a volume to a number of particles?
  • What is the mass of half a litre of water, and why is that number so convenient?
  • What is the molar mass of water, and can you get it from the periodic table in your head?
Anchors
density of water = 1 g cm−3M(H₂O) = 18 g mol−1NA = 6 × 1023
One route
m = 500 g → n = 500 ÷ 18 ≈ 28 mol
N = n NA = 28 × 6 × 1023
N ≈ 2 × 1025 molecules

More molecules in the glass than there are glasses of water in every ocean. Worth checking that claim yourself.

Fermi 05 · Deep Time

How long would it take to drive to the nearest star in a family car?

Ask yourself
  • How far is a light year in metres, and can you build it from c and the seconds in a year?
  • What is seventy miles per hour in SI units, to one significant figure?
  • Is the answer better expressed in seconds, years, or in comparison with something historical?
Anchors
c = 3 × 108 m s−11 yr ≈ 3 × 107 sProxima ≈ 4 ly70 mph ≈ 30 m s−1
One route
1 ly = 3 × 108 × 3 × 107 ≈ 1016 m → d ≈ 4 × 1016 m
t = 4 × 1016 ÷ 30 ≈ 1 × 1015 s
t ≈ 4 × 107 years

Longer than the entire history of the primates. The number is useless as a travel plan and excellent as a sense of scale.

Fermi 06 · Packing

How many tennis balls could you fit inside Centre Court at Wimbledon?

Ask yourself
  • What shape are you approximating the stadium as, and does the roof count?
  • Should a ball be treated as a sphere or as the cube that contains it? What error does each choice introduce?
  • Do spheres fill space completely? By roughly what fraction are you over-counting?
Anchors
stadium ≈ 100 × 100 × 10 mball diameter ≈ 7 cmclose packing fills ≈ 74 %
One route
Vcourt ≈ 102 × 102 × 10 = 105 m³
treat each ball as a 10 cm cube → Vball = 10−3 m³
N ≈ 1 × 108 balls

Using real dimensions, 110 × 119 × 19 m, and a 6.6 cm ball gives 8.7 × 108. Nine times larger, and still the same order of magnitude once you round. That gap is the lesson.

Why This Works At All

A Fermi estimate multiplies half a dozen guesses, each of which might be wrong by a factor of two or three. It has no right to land anywhere near the answer, and yet it usually does.

log N  =  log a  +  log b  +  log c  +  … Errors add in logarithms, so they add in quadrature rather than accumulating

If each factor is independently wrong by up to a factor of three, and you use six of them, the total error grows as the square root of six rather than as three to the sixth power. Over-estimates and under-estimates cancel. This only holds while the guesses are independent, which is why the dangerous error is the systematic one you make in every term at once.

Fermi 07 · School Scale

How many metres of spaghetti would be needed if every child in the school had spaghetti bolognese for lunch?

Ask yourself
  • How many strands are in a portion? Could you count a small handful and scale?
  • Is dry length the same as cooked length?
  • Which is easier, mass per portion and mass per metre, or counting strands directly?
Anchors
school ≈ 700 pupilsportion ≈ 100 g drystrand ≈ 0.25 m, ≈ 0.5 g
One route
strands per portion = 100 ÷ 0.5 = 200
length per portion = 200 × 0.25 = 50 m
total = 700 × 50
L ≈ 4 × 104 m  ≈  35 km

Roughly the distance across London twice. This one is genuinely checkable: weigh a strand, and see how badly the anchor was wrong.

Fermi 08 · Density

If everyone in the world moved to Wales, how crowded would it be?

Ask yourself
  • Crowded compared with what? Pick a comparison before you calculate, or the number will mean nothing.
  • Are you asking about area per person, or about whether they would physically fit?
  • How large is Wales, and how would you estimate it from a map you half remember?
Anchors
Wales ≈ 2 × 104 km²population ≈ 8 × 109crowded concert ≈ 4 people m−2
One route
A = 2 × 104 km² = 2 × 1010 m²
area each = 2 × 1010 ÷ 8 × 109
≈ 2.5 m² per person

Everyone fits, standing comfortably, with room to turn around. The whole species occupies an area smaller than a small country. Now ask why that fact does not settle any argument about resources.

Fermi 09 · Thin Films

What total distance will a single ballpoint pen write before it runs out?

Ask yourself
  • What is conserved here? Which quantity is fixed at the start and used up steadily?
  • What are the dimensions of the ink column inside the tube?
  • How wide is a line, and how thick is the deposited film? Which of those two do you know worse?
Anchors
ink column ≈ 10 cm × 2 mm diameterline width ≈ 0.5 mmfilm thickness ≈ 1 μm
One route
Vink ≈ π(10−3)² × 0.1 ≈ 3 × 10−7 m³
cross-section of the written line ≈ 5 × 10−4 × 10−6 = 5 × 10−10 m²
L = V ÷ A
L ≈ 600 m

Manufacturers quote figures in the same range. The film thickness was the shakiest anchor and it sits in the denominator, so it dominates the error.

Fermi 10 · Two Directions

How many hairdressers and barber shops are there in the United Kingdom?

Ask yourself
  • Top down: how much haircutting does the country demand, and how much can one shop supply?
  • Bottom up: how many can you name within a mile, and how many such areas are there?
  • The two routes share almost no assumptions. What does it mean if they agree?
Anchors
UK ≈ 6.7 × 107≈ 6 haircuts per person per year≈ 8 cuts per chair per day≈ 3 chairs per shop≈ 300 open days
One route
demand = 6.7 × 107 × 6 ≈ 4 × 108 cuts / yr
supply per shop = 8 × 3 × 300 ≈ 7 × 103 cuts / yr
N ≈ 6 × 104 shops

Bottom up: perhaps three shops in a neighbourhood of ten thousand people, giving about twenty thousand. The two disagree by a factor of three, which is agreement by Fermi standards, and the gap itself is informative about idle capacity.

Top Down and Bottom Up

Top down

  • Start with a large aggregate you know reasonably well.
  • Divide it by a rate, a share, or a capacity.
  • Fails when the aggregate hides variation you have averaged away.

Bottom up

  • Start with one unit you can genuinely observe.
  • Multiply up through a hierarchy of containment.
  • Fails when your one unit is unrepresentative, and you never find out.

Doing both is not twice the work. It is the only check you have.

Fermi 11 · Sampling a Surface

How many hairs are there on your head? And at what number would you call someone bald?

Ask yourself
  • Could you count hairs in one square centimetre and scale up? What is the ecological version of that technique?
  • What is the area of a scalp, and what simple solid are you approximating a head as?
  • The second question is not a measurement question at all. What kind of question is it?
Anchors
head ≈ sphere of radius 9 cmscalp ≈ half that surfacedensity ≈ 200 hairs cm−2
One route
Asphere = 4πr² ≈ 4π(9)² ≈ 1000 cm² → scalp ≈ 500 cm²
N = 500 × 200
N ≈ 1 × 105 hairs

The baldness question has no numerical answer, because "bald" is a vague predicate rather than a measured quantity. Asking students where the boundary sits, and watching them fail to agree, is the point.

Fermi 12 · Mixing

How many of the atoms in your body once belonged to a person who has died?

Ask yourself
  • What reservoir have those atoms been mixed back into, and is it the whole Earth or something much smaller?
  • How many people have ever lived, and how does that compare with the number now alive?
  • What assumption about mixing are you making, and is it remotely defensible over a few thousand years?
Anchors
you ≈ 1029 atoms≈ 1011 humans have ever livedbiosphere-accessible reservoir, not the whole 1050 atoms of Earth
One route
all humans ever ≈ 1011 × 1029 = 1040 atoms
if the mixing reservoir is the biosphere and near-surface water, of order 1044 atoms
fraction of your atoms once in a dead human ≈ 1040 ÷ 1044
≈ 10−4, so roughly 1025 of your atoms

The answer swings by many orders of magnitude depending on the reservoir you choose, and choosing the reservoir is the whole physics. Compare with taking the whole Earth, 1050 atoms, and watch the answer collapse.

Fermi 13 · Energy Conversion

How much heat is generated by rain striking the ground in Trafalgar Square during a typical thunderstorm?

Ask yourself
  • Where does the energy come from, and what happens to it when a drop lands?
  • Do you need the speed of the drops, or does the height they fell from suffice?
  • How much rain falls in a storm, expressed as a depth over an area?
Anchors
square ≈ 100 × 100 mrainfall ≈ 20 mmcloud base ≈ 1000 mg = 10 m s−2
One route
V = 104 m² × 0.02 m = 200 m³ → m = 2 × 105 kg
E = mgh = 2 × 105 × 10 × 103
E ≈ 2 × 109 J

Most of that is lost to air resistance long before impact, so this is an upper bound on the gravitational budget rather than the heat delivered at the ground. Saying which one you have computed is worth more than the number.

Fermi 14 · Non-Constant Rates

How long would it take to count aloud to one million?

Ask yourself
  • Does one number take the same time to say as another? Say "seven" and then "seven hundred and forty-three thousand, two hundred and eighteen".
  • What fraction of the numbers below a million have six digits?
  • Are you counting continuously, or sleeping? State the convention before you compute.
Anchors
a six-digit number ≈ 3 s to say90 % of them have six digits1 day = 8.6 × 104 s
One route
t ≈ 106 × 3 s ≈ 3 × 106 s
at 8 hours a day: 3 × 106 ÷ (3 × 104)
≈ 35 days non-stop, or about 3 months at 8 hours a day

The naive version, one number per second, is wrong by a factor of three because the mean cost per item is not the cost of a typical early item. That failure mode recurs everywhere in estimation.

Fermi 15 · Quadrat Sampling

How many blades of grass are there on a football pitch?

Ask yourself
  • What is the smallest area you could realistically count, and how would you place it?
  • Where on the pitch would you place your quadrats, and why does that choice matter more than the counting?
  • How many square metres is a pitch, to one significant figure?
Anchors
pitch ≈ 100 × 70 m≈ 10 blades cm−21 m² = 104 cm²
One route
A ≈ 7 × 103 m² = 7 × 107 cm²
N = 7 × 107 × 10
N ≈ 7 × 108 blades

This is the only question here you could actually verify, by counting a quadrat and scaling. It is also the one where the sampling design, rather than the arithmetic, determines whether the answer is any good.

Thinking Like a Scientist

The point is never the number. It is whether you can build a route to one.

Make links between the world of ideas and the world of observations. Use a model, know that it is a model, and say which of your assumptions the answer is most sensitive to. Then say how confident you are, and why.

An estimate you can defend is worth more than a figure you cannot explain.
