We Were Grading the Mirror

What AI has revealed about school “analysis”
Lee McCulloch-James

The machine’s fluency reads like a confession extracted from our assignments.

Much of what schools call analysis has always been derivative. The English paragraph names a technique and supplies its effect. The history response applies an established interpretation to selected evidence. The science evaluation proceeds through criteria already rehearsed. The mathematical solution recognises a familiar structure and reconstructs a taught method.

The framework, vocabulary, criteria and acceptable lines of inference have usually arrived from outside. What the student supplies is the labour of retention and the fluency of the mirror: I heard what you said, absorbed it and can now reconstitute it on demand.

This has educational value. Apprenticeship into a discipline begins through inherited ways of seeing. Students learn how a physicist idealises, how a historian weighs a source, how a literary critic attends to language and how a mathematician recognises structure.

The misdescription begins when successful reconstitution is presented as independent critical judgement.

Because the student’s labour was visible and effortful, we mistook it for the thing itself. We graded the mirror and called the reflection analysis.

AI has made that mistake harder to sustain. A language model can produce the analytical paragraph, apply the supplied framework and generate the expected evaluative phrase with unnerving ease. Its success tells us something about the task we designed. The assessment accepted the outward form of discernment as evidence that the inner act had occurred.

What the machine has confessed about school analysis is uncomfortable: much of it was recombination wearing the clothes of discernment.

The profession’s response has been remarkably light-touch:

“Schools must promote critical thinking in the age of AI.”

Repeated often enough, the sentence becomes a small act of cognitive offloading itself. It relieves us of specifying what critical thinking consists of, where it occurs, and how we will know that the student is doing it.

We are told that AI will handle routine work and free students for higher-order thought. A wonderfully convenient account, since selection, compression, connection, evaluation and judgement often appear among the routine work being handed over.

Pressure to keep pace now performs the same substitution as the old analytical assignment. A professionally recognisable response takes the place of an interrogated one.

Fear does not interrogate. It adopts.

The substitution has occurred twice. First, the polished analytical paragraph stood in for discernment. Now the polished AI-assisted workflow stands in for an account of what intellectual work remains with the learner.

If the finished paragraph was largely an act of reconstitution, discernment may have occurred earlier and attracted far less professional attention. It may have occurred during the lesson, while the student decided what deserved to be noticed and written down:

  • What is central here?
  • Which example reveals the principle?
  • Which detail can be omitted?
  • What connects with something said ten minutes ago?
  • What remains unclear?

This quiet act was relegated to preparation. We treated the rough notes from which an answer emerged as classroom housekeeping. Now note-making is among the first activities we are willing to give to AI.

Writing is certainly a cognitive offload. The page retains information that the mind no longer has to hold. Note-making can also impose a productive cognitive demand. The student must select, compress, organise and decide what matters. Writing moves memory out of the head while drawing judgement into action.

A student may fill six pages while making almost no decisions. The hand moves, the page fills and the lesson passes. Another may record half a page containing the governing relationship, one revealing example and a genuine question. The quantity of writing tells us very little about the thinking involved.

Moving upstream does not sanctify the notebook. A copied page is another mirror. A notebook becomes evidence of discernment only where it records choices: emphasis, omission, connection, uncertainty and the first attempt to distinguish signal from noise.

An AI-generated summary can preserve the content and perform those choices. Its accuracy tells us nothing about the student’s capacity to make them.

One cannot offload a judgement one has never made.

Discernment has a special educational status. A machine may make an excellent judgement about relevance. That judgement cannot count as the student’s. Once the deciding of what matters has moved elsewhere, no comparable student act remains from which discernment can be inferred.

At this point, one might expect education to possess a careful developmental account of note-making: how much selection a twelve-year-old can manage, how much structure a sixteen-year-old should supply, and how that responsibility should grow across a 45-minute lesson.

It does not.

The research tells us broad things. Kobayashi’s 2005 meta-analysis found a small average benefit from taking notes, while his 2006 analysis found a much larger benefit when students subsequently reviewed them. Research with Grade 7 students found that explicit instruction helped students use fewer words while capturing more principal concepts. Recent high-school mathematics research found value in recording meaning, worked solutions and points of uncertainty.

These clues stop far short of a developmental account. There is no defensible optimum expressed in words, pages or minutes, and most controlled research still uses undergraduates. We cannot say how much of the selecting, compressing and organising a thirteen-year-old should carry. We are nevertheless moving those acts into AI systems under the banner of freeing the student to think.

That gap continues the indictment. After years of calling the finished response “analysis”, we failed to specify how discernment develops in the quieter act that precedes it. Now we risk automating that act before we have learned to recognise it.

The rough notebook offers evidence only when its emphases, omissions, connections and questions belong to the student. The profession may have protected the finished mirror while neglecting the formation of the gaze.

If the AI, teacher or textbook has already decided what matters, what exactly is the student being freed to think critically about?
