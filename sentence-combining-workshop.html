The Combining Room

Sentence-combining tasks for physics, in which the object of the exercise is not a longer sentence but a truer one. Subordinate clauses encode conditions, appositives classify nouns, and the passive voice quietly decides which interacting system the reader is allowed to see.

Lee McCulloch-James · Ontological fluency in school physics

Combining sentences changes what is foregrounded, what is subordinated, and which entity appears to act.

Treated as style, this is a literacy exercise that happens to use physics vocabulary. Treated as physics, it is modelling made visible at sentence level: every clause boundary is a decision about what belongs to the system, every appositive is a claim about what kind of thing a term denotes, and every passive construction is an opportunity to delete a second interacting body without anyone noticing.

Each task below asks students to write first and compare afterwards. The comparison matters more than the match, since two defensible sentences that foreground different things are exactly the material the follow-up questions are for.

A fifth task type, clause reordering, sits beside the other four. Two scientifically accurate sentences can imply different causal priorities depending on which clause is subordinated, and the exercise is to notice the difference rather than to prefer either version outright.

The sequence, in five moves

Students receive short factual sentences; combine them using clauses, appositives or passive constructions; identify what the syntax has foregrounded or suppressed; inspect the implied ontology; and rewrite to restore the physical relations, or, given two accurate orderings already, to weigh which causal priority each one implies.

The disciplinary purpose is what separates this from a grammar drill. Students are learning to write longer scientific sentences, and learning at the same time that grammatical structure carries a model of what exists, what acts, and what depends upon what.

Written to be used aloud. The model answers are not the only defensible combinations, and where a class produces a better one the follow-up questions still apply.
